The Auction

Forty thousand advertisers, three slots, and nobody willing to say what a click is really worth to them. The auction's job is to allocate the slots well and set fair prices, all at once.

Why not just set a price list, say $1 per click, and let advertisers queue? Because Pebble doesn't know what a click is worth. To Nimbus, the neobank, a click that turns into an account might be worth $10. To Orbit, a mobile game, a click might be worth 30 cents. Each advertiser knows its own value and has every reason to keep it private. An auction is a mechanism for getting them to reveal it through their bids.

2.1First price or second price?

Start with the simplest case: one slot, and every candidate has the same click probability. Each advertiser submits a sealed bid. The highest bid wins. The question is what the winner pays.

  • In a first-price auction, the winner pays its own bid.
  • In a second-price auction, the winner pays the second-highest bid, the least it could have bid and still won.

First price sounds like it earns more. But advertisers aren't naive. If you pay your bid, you'll bid less than your value (this is called bid shading), and guessing how much to shade becomes a strategic game. Try it yourself. You're Trailhead, and you know what a click is worth to you. The other bidders' behaviour is fixed. Find the bid that maximizes your expected surplus: value minus price, averaged over many auctions.

Intuition

In a second-price auction, bidding your true value is the best strategy, whatever everyone else does. Your bid only decides whether you win, never what you pay. Bidding above your value risks winning at a price above what the click is worth to you. Bidding below it risks losing auctions you'd have profited from. Economists call this property truthfulness (or incentive compatibility). It makes life simpler for advertisers, and it lets the platform learn what clicks are really worth.

In a single-slot second-price auction, Nimbus values a click at $4.00. The other bids are $2.50, $3.10, and $1.80. What should Nimbus bid, and what does it pay if it wins?

  • Bid $3.11, pay $3.11
  • Bid $4.00, pay $3.10
  • Bid $4.00, pay $4.00
  • Bid $4.00, pay $1.80

Bid the true value, $4.00. It wins and pays the second-highest bid, $3.10, for a surplus of $0.90. Any bid above $3.10 gives the same outcome, which is exactly why there's no point shading.

2.2Ranking by expected value: eCPM

Now drop the assumption that every ad is equally likely to be clicked. Pebble charges per click (this is CPC bidding), but it sells impressions. An ad bidding $5 per click that nobody clicks earns nothing. So the auction ranks by expected revenue per impression:

\[ \text{score} = \text{bid} \times \text{pCTR}, \qquad \text{eCPM} = \text{bid} \times \text{pCTR} \times 1000 \]

eCPM ("effective cost per mille") is the same score expressed per thousand impressions, the industry's common currency. It lets you compare an ad that bids per click with one that bids per impression or per conversion. This is the moment predictions enter the auction. Pebble's revenue, and each advertiser's position, now depend on pCTR as much as on bids.

Sprout bids $1.20 per click and its predicted CTR is 2.5%. What's its eCPM?

Hint

bid × pCTR × 1000, with pCTR as a probability (0.025).

1.20 × 0.025 × 1000 = $30. Pebble expects to earn $30 for every thousand times it shows this ad.

2.3Many slots: the generalized second-price auction

A Pebble feed load has three ad slots, and they aren't equal. Users see the first more often than the third (Chapter 5 measures exactly how much). The classic design for multiple slots is the generalized second-price auction (GSP), used for decades in search and feed ads:

  1. Rank candidates by score = bid × pCTR.
  2. The top-ranked ad gets slot 1, the next gets slot 2, and so on.
  3. Each winner pays, per click, the least it could have bid and kept its position. That's the score of the ad just below it, divided by its own pCTR:
\[ \text{price}_i = \frac{\text{bid}_{i+1} \cdot \text{pCTR}_{i+1}}{\text{pCTR}_i} \]

Read that formula carefully. Your price depends on your own pCTR. If the model thinks your ad is more clickable, you pay less per click for the same position. That's intended: it rewards relevant ads. It also means that when the model is wrong about your ad, you're mispriced. That's the whole subject of Chapter 3.

Hint

Scores: 2.00×0.03, 3.00×0.015, 1.00×0.04. The winner pays the runner-up's score divided by its own pCTR.

Scores: Lumen 0.060, Orbit 0.045, Trailhead 0.040. Lumen wins and pays 0.045 / 0.03 = $1.50 per click. Notice that Orbit bid the most and still lost. Its ad is predicted to be half as clickable as Lumen's.

Going deeper: GSP isn't truthful, and VCG is

With multiple slots, GSP loses the second-price auction's magic property. An advertiser can sometimes gain by bidding below value to drop to a cheaper slot. The VCG mechanism (Vickrey–Clarke–Groves) charges each winner the harm its presence does to everyone else. That makes truthful bidding optimal again, and some large platforms have used VCG-style pricing for exactly this reason. In practice the difference matters less than you'd think. Most advertisers now use autobidding, where the platform bids on their behalf toward a goal like "maximize conversions at a $40 target cost," and much modern auction research is about designing well in that world. The classic analysis is by Edelman, Ostrovsky and Schwarz (2007) and by Varian (2007).

Common trap

Ranking by bid alone. It sounds fair ("highest bidder wins"), and it's what people implement first. But it hands slots to ads that nobody clicks. That earns less for Pebble, wastes users' attention, and invites low-quality advertisers to buy their way to the top. 2.5Reserve prices: the platform's lever

When few advertisers compete for a request, second prices can be very low. A lone bidder pays nothing at all. A reserve price sets a floor: an ad must have eCPM of at least the reserve to be shown, and the last winner pays at least the reserve. Raise it and prices go up, but some slots go unfilled. Those empty slots mean lost revenue, though they also mean fewer weak ads in front of users.

Run your own stack, with your auction, at different reserve prices over a day of Pebble traffic. Then run the sweep again in a thin market, where only two ads compete for three slots. That's what a niche audience or a newly launched surface looks like. Then choose a reserve for production.

In your job

Reserve prices are one of the few marketplace levers that change prices directly, so they get argued over. Sales teams worry about advertisers who stop spending, finance likes the revenue, and quality teams like the fewer weak ads. Two things make a good reserve policy. It's usually personalized (different floors for different users, surfaces, or countries, learned from data). And it's evaluated on long-run advertiser retention, not just next week's revenue. An advertiser priced out today might not come back.

You raise Pebble's reserve and revenue goes up 4% in a week-long A/B test. Which effect would the test most likely miss?

  • Fewer slots being filled
  • Higher prices per click
  • Small advertisers slowly reducing budgets or churning because their costs rose
  • Users seeing fewer ads

Advertisers respond slowly. Budgets are set monthly, and campaigns get reviewed by humans or autobidders over weeks. A short test sees the price increase but not the demand response. Marketplace teams use longer holdouts and advertiser-level metrics (spend retention, ROI) for exactly this reason. Chapter 14 comes back to short-term versus long-term.

2.6What advertisers actually bid for

So far every advertiser bids per click. Real platforms offer several bid types, each a promise about what the advertiser pays for:

CPMthousand impressionsbidnone (for pricing)
CPCclickbid × pCTR × 1000pCTR
CPA / oCPMconversion (or impression, optimized for conversions)bid × pCTR × pCVR × 1000pCTR and pCVR

Moving down the table, the advertiser takes on less risk and Pebble takes on more. Every step down needs another accurate prediction. Your stack uses CPC for now. In Chapter 12 you'll build a conversion model and switch on oCPM.

Two weeks after your team launched a new pCTR model, Lumen's account manager escalates: Lumen's cost per click rose 20%, even though their bids didn't change. Nothing else changed in the marketplace. Using the GSP price formula, give two different explanations, and say what data you'd pull to tell them apart.

From price = (runner-up's bid × runner-up's pCTR) / Lumen's pCTR, the price rises if the numerator rises or the denominator falls.

  • The model predicts a lower pCTR for Lumen's ads. Maybe the new model thinks Lumen's creatives are less clickable. If it's right, Lumen was being undercharged before. If it's wrong (for example, a feature bug for Lumen's category), Lumen is now overcharged. Pull Lumen's average pCTR before and after, next to their actual CTR: the calibration of predictions against outcomes for Lumen's ads.
  • The model predicts a higher pCTR for Lumen's competitors. The runner-up's score rose, so Lumen pays more to keep the same position. Pull the scores of the ads ranked just below Lumen's.
  • A composition shift. Lumen may now win different impressions (different users or slots), where prices are naturally higher. Compare like with like: the same slot, and similar users.

The underlying point, which Chapter 3 makes precise, is that a pCTR model change is a pricing change. That's why launches get reviewed for per-advertiser calibration, not just overall AUC.
