Chapter 9 · Part III · Learning

Evaluation, Offline and Online

Offline metrics tell you whether a model learned something. Only an experiment tells you whether it's worth shipping, and the two disagree more often than anyone likes.

You have a trained logistic regression sitting in your stack, and a heuristic serving production. This chapter gives you the tools to decide whether to swap them. You'll build the two metrics every ads ranking review opens with, read them against the simulator's truth, slice them, and then run the real test: a randomized A/B experiment on your own traffic. At the end you make the ship decision.

9.1AUC: can the model rank?

AUC (area under the ROC curve) has a definition that sounds technical and a meaning that's simple: pick a random clicked impression and a random non-clicked one. AUC is the probability the model scored the clicked one higher. A coin flip gets 0.5 and a perfect ranker gets 1.0. Pebble's own truth only reaches about 0.85, because clicks are random even when you know the exact probabilities.

Two properties make AUC popular. It ignores calibration completely (only the order matters), and it doesn't depend on the base rate. Two properties make it dangerous. It ignores calibration completely, and it averages over every pair, including pairs the auction never compares, like two different users at two different times.

9.2Normalized entropy: are the numbers right?

Log loss measures probabilities, but its raw value depends on the base rate: a 1% CTR dataset has lower log loss than a 10% one, however good the model. Normalized entropy (NE), popularized by Facebook's ads team (He et al., 2014), divides the model's log loss by the log loss of the laziest honest model, the one that always predicts the average CTR:

\[ \text{NE} = \frac{-\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \big[y_i \log \hat p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat p_i)\big]}{-\big[\bar y \log \bar y + (1-\bar y)\log(1-\bar y)\big]} \]

NE = 1 means "no better than predicting the average." Lower is better. Because log loss punishes miscalibration, NE captures ranking and calibration together. That makes it the natural headline metric for a model whose outputs become prices.

A dataset has a 10% click rate. Your model's average log loss on it is 0.29. What's its NE?

Hint

Denominator: −(0.1 ln 0.1 + 0.9 ln 0.9).

−(0.1 × −2.303 + 0.9 × −0.105) = 0.325, and 0.29 / 0.325 ≈ 0.892. The model removes about 11% of the uncertainty that predicting the average leaves. In mature ads systems, launches are often decided on NE improvements of a tenth of a percent.

Common trap

Evaluating on a random sample of the training period instead of a later one. Ads data drifts (Chapter 13) and has leaky time structure (Chapter 6), so a random split flatters the model. Always evaluate on data from after the training window, the way it'll be used.

9.5The offline–online gap

Offline evaluation has a blind spot built in. It measures the model on impressions the old system chose to show. Once the new model is in charge, it will score ~150 candidates per request, most of which the old system never showed, so there are no labels for them. If the model is badly wrong about those unseen candidates, offline metrics can't tell you. Only serving it can. This is the offline–online gap, and the exposure bias from Chapter 5 is its most common cause.

9.6The A/B test

The only way to measure what a model does in production is to put it in production for a random subset of users. In an A/B test, users are split at random, by a hash of their user ID, into a control arm (the current system) and a treatment arm (the new model). Both arms see the same days, the same advertisers, the same world. Any difference beyond chance is caused by the model.

"Beyond chance" is the hard part. Revenue per user is noisy: most users click nothing, and a few click a lot. The widget reports a 95% confidence interval for each lift, computed by resampling users (a bootstrap). If the interval includes zero, you can't rule out that the difference is noise. And because this is a simulation, it also shows the true expected lift, the number the experiment is trying to estimate.

Run an experiment on your stack
Control: your heuristic. Treatment: your logistic regression. Three days, 4,000 requests a day, users split 50/50.
Intuition

Run an A/A test (both arms identical) and you'll still see "lifts" of several percent. That's the noise floor. A real effect has to clear it. Real ads experiments run for weeks on millions of users to detect lifts of a fraction of a percent. Your three-day test on a few thousand simulated users can only detect big effects.

Your new model improves offline AUC from 0.742 to 0.748. In a two-week A/B test, revenue per user is −0.4% with a 95% CI of [−1.1%, +0.3%]. What's the right reading?

  • The model hurts revenue; roll it back
  • The model helps revenue
  • No detectable revenue effect; if revenue was the goal, the offline gain didn't translate. Check other metrics and why before deciding
  • Ship it, since AUC is up

The test can't distinguish the effect from zero. Before deciding, check guardrail metrics (user experience, advertiser value, latency) and whether the offline gain came from somewhere the auction doesn't care about. For example, a better ranking of pairs across users doesn't change who wins any auction.

An A/B test shows CTR +6% (significant) but advertiser conversions −3% (significant). What's the most likely story?

  • Measurement noise
  • The model favours ads that get clicked but don't convert, such as attention-grabbing creatives
  • Users clicked more, so they must have bought more
  • Advertisers ran out of budget

A click model optimizes clicks. If the ads that gain delivery are clickbait-like (high CTR, low CVR), clicks rise while conversions fall. Advertisers pay more per conversion and eventually bid less. That's why advertiser value is a guardrail in ads experiments, and why Chapter 12 adds a conversion model.

Example structure (your numbers will differ): "We recommend launching LR-v1 as the Pebble pCTR ranker. Offline, on held-out day 10, AUC improves from 0.64 to 0.74 and NE from 1.00 to 0.91, with calibration of 0.98 overall and within ±10% in every category slice except Travel (1.14, low volume). In a 3-day A/B test (≈2,000 users per arm), true revenue per request is +X% [CI a, b] and CTR +Y% [CI]. Advertiser conversion value is flat [CI], so there's no evidence the model trades conversions for clicks. Risks: the model was trained on logs with Z% exploration, so behaviour on rarely shown ads is less certain; new-ad calibration should be monitored for 2 weeks; the test was short, so long-run advertiser response is unmeasured. We'll keep a 5% holdout on the heuristic for 4 weeks."
