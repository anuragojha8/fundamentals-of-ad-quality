Chapter 14 · Part V · The Job

Quality Beyond the Click

Pebble's users didn't open the app to see ads. Every ad spends a little of their patience. The quality PM's real job is to spend it wisely.

Everything so far has optimized clicks, conversions, and revenue. Those are what advertisers pay for, and they're measurable within a day. But ads have a cost that doesn't show up in the auction: an irrelevant or annoying ad makes Pebble a little worse. Users hide ads, scroll faster, open the app less. Those effects are slow, diffuse, and easy to ignore, right up until they aren't. This chapter puts them into your stack's objective.

14.1The objective you actually own

A healthy ads marketplace balances three parties: advertisers (value per dollar), users (experience), and the platform (revenue, now and later). Many large platforms rank ads by some version of total value:

\[ \text{score} = \underbrace{\text{bid} \times \text{pCTR} \,(\times \text{pCVR})}_{\text{advertiser value}} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{quality}}_{\text{user value}} \;-\; \underbrace{\lambda \cdot \text{pHide} \cdot c}_{\text{user cost}} \]

Two numbers in that formula aren't predictions. They're policy: the weight \(\lambda\) on user cost, and how many ad slots to show at all (ad load). No model can learn them from clicks. They encode how much short-term revenue Pebble will trade for long-term user health, and setting them well is one of the most consequential things an ads quality PM does.

14.2Predicting annoyance

Pebble lets users hide an ad ("not interested"). Hides are the clearest negative signal you get, and they're rare: about one per 180 impressions in your logs. In Pebble's world, hides come mostly from ads that don't match the user's interests and from "annoying" creatives, which are a trait of some advertisers. Train a pHide model with the same recipe as your pCTR model: same features, same trainer, different label.

14.3Pricing annoyance into the auction

Now add the penalty: each ad's score becomes bid × pCTR − λ · pHide. Your stack implements this by passing the auction a quality-adjusted pCTR, so the penalty affects both who wins and what they pay. Sweep λ and look at the frontier.

Intuition

The first units of quality penalty are often free, or better than free. Ads that users hide are usually ads they wouldn't click either, so demoting them makes room for ads that earn more. Past some point the penalty starts removing ads that make money and are only somewhat annoying. That's where the real tradeoff begins, and where the PM has to decide.

14.4Ad load and the long run

The biggest lever of all is how many ads to show. More slots means more revenue today, almost linearly. The cost arrives later: users who see more ads, and more hideable ones, come back a little less often. Measuring that effect in real life takes long-running holdout experiments: a small group of users kept on lower ad load for months, compared with everyone else. Pebble's simulator doesn't model user churn, so the widget below uses a simple, explicit toy model. You set how sensitive users are, which is exactly the number a long-term holdout would estimate.

Short-term revenue vs. a year
Revenue and hides per request come from your stack (one simulated day per ad load). User retention is a toy model: weekly user loss = sensitivity × (0.8% × ad slots + 60% × hides per request), against 1% weekly organic growth.
Common trap

Deciding ad load with a two-week A/B test. Revenue responds instantly, while user behaviour takes months to adjust, and both arms share the same app-wide trends. Short tests systematically favour more ads. Mature platforms keep long-term holdouts, model the long-term effect of short-term metrics (sometimes called surrogate or proxy metrics), and set explicit guardrails on user-experience metrics before launching anything that adds ads.

Adding a hide penalty (λ = 1) raised revenue and reduced hides in your simulation. Why can that happen?

  • The penalty only changes prices
  • Ads likely to be hidden are often also unlikely to be clicked, so demoting them promotes ads that earn more
  • Advertisers raised their bids
  • Pebble showed more ads

When pCTR is imperfect, pHide carries extra information about relevance. Early quality penalties partly correct ranking mistakes. The tradeoff only appears once the penalty starts removing ads that are genuinely profitable.

Finance proposes raising Pebble's ad load from 1 ad in 8 posts to 1 in 6, projecting +18% revenue. Write your response: what you'd need to see, how you'd test it, and what guardrails you'd set.

  • Expected vs. measured: +18% assumes ads in the new slots monetize like existing ones. They usually don't: marginal slots are lower in the feed, get less attention, and dilute advertiser value. Expect less.
  • The long-term cost: ask for evidence from past load changes or existing long-term holdouts. What happened to sessions per user, retention, and time spent 8–12 weeks later? Translate that into revenue lost over a year, since fewer sessions means fewer ads.
  • Test design: a long-running holdout (months) at the higher load, plus a smaller-step ramp (1 in 7 first). Measure user metrics weekly, not just revenue.
  • Guardrails: set predefined stop conditions, for example hide rate +X%, sessions −Y%, retention −Z%. Also consider quality-aware load: show extra ads only where high-quality candidates exist (and to users who tolerate them), rather than a flat increase.
  • Advertiser side: more inventory can lower prices (more supply, same demand), so revenue per ad falls, which is another reason the +18% is optimistic.

What you built

A pHide model, a quality penalty in your auction with a λ you chose, and an ad-load decision made with an explicit long-term assumption. The capstone will hold you to both.
