Chapter 12 · Part IV · The Models

Conversion Modeling

Advertisers don't want clicks. They want customers. Predicting conversions is harder in every way: the labels are rare, late, and only exist for the users who clicked.

Every ad your stack has served so far was sold per click. But a click is only a means to an end. Sprout wants meal-kit subscriptions, Nimbus wants bank accounts, Orbit wants installs. Modern ad platforms let advertisers bid for the outcome directly and take on the job of predicting it. This chapter builds the conversion model (pCVR) your stack needs for that, and walks through the three problems that make it hard: sparse labels, delayed labels, and labels that only exist after a click.

12.1Bidding for outcomes

With oCPM (optimized CPM) or conversion bidding, an advertiser states a target cost per acquisition (CPA): "I'll pay up to $40 per new customer." The platform turns that into an auction score by predicting both steps:

\[ \text{eCPM} = \text{CPA}_{\text{target}} \times \text{pCTR} \times \text{pCVR} \times 1000 \]

where pCVR = P(conversion | click). The advertiser no longer needs to know its own conversion rate. The platform effectively bids on its behalf, which is the essence of autobidding. In exchange, the platform's predictions now directly set how much the advertiser pays per conversion. If pCVR is too high, the advertiser overpays. If it's too low, they under-deliver and leave.

Sprout's target CPA is $40. For some impression, pCTR = 2% and pCVR = 5%. What's the eCPM?

Hint

40 × 0.02 × 0.05 × 1000.

40 × 0.02 × 0.05 × 1000 = $40. Per impression, Sprout is expected to be worth 4 cents to Pebble: a 1-in-1,000 chance of a conversion worth $40. Note that eCPM is the product of two predictions, so a 20% error in each compounds to about 44%.

12.2Sparse labels

Your ten days of logs hold about 180,000 impressions, roughly 7,000 clicks, and only a few hundred conversions. That's one conversion per ~350 impressions. A model with a weight per ad ID has, on average, one or two conversions per ad to learn from. This is why conversion models lean heavily on shared signal: category, advertiser, and user-level features, plus tricks that borrow strength from the plentiful click task (Section 12.4).

12.3Delayed labels

A click on a Gaming ad converts within minutes. A click on Nimbus's bank ad converts within days, if it converts at all. In Pebble's world the average delay ranges from 2 hours (Gaming) to 5 days (Finance). When your training job runs, the most recent clicks haven't had time to convert. You saw this in Chapter 4: they're labeled 0 when they're really "not yet."

If delays follow a known distribution, you can correct for it. Assuming exponential delays with mean \(\mu\), the fraction of eventual conversions already observed \(t\) hours after the click is

\[ F(t) = 1 - e^{-t/\mu} \]

So instead of dividing observed conversions by clicks, divide by the expected share of clicks that have had time to convert: \( \widehat{\text{CVR}} = \frac{\text{conversions observed}}{\sum_{\text{clicks}} F(t_{\text{now}} - t_{\text{click}})} \).

Delayed feedback is behind many "the model under-predicts for advertiser X" escalations. Advertisers with long conversion cycles (finance, travel, B2B, high-consideration purchases) are systematically under-predicted by models trained on fresh data, so they under-deliver. Ask how the conversion model handles delay. Common answers: wait N days before using data (stale but clean), weight or correct for expected delay, or model the delay distribution explicitly (Chapelle, 2014, "Modeling Delayed Feedback in Display Advertising"). And ask how conversions that arrive after the attribution window are handled.

12.4Sample selection bias and ESMM

pCVR is defined as P(conversion | click). The obvious way to train it is on clicked impressions only. But the auction needs pCVR for every candidate, clicked or not, because it multiplies pCTR × pCVR before anyone has clicked. Clicked impressions aren't a random sample of candidates. Users who click a lot, and creatives that attract clicks, are over-represented, and in Pebble's world (as in reality) clicky users and clickbait creatives convert less. A model trained only on clicks learns the conversion rate of the click-happy, then gets applied to everyone. That's sample selection bias.

ESMM (Ma et al., 2018) fixes this with a multi-task trick from Chapter 8. It has two heads, pCTR and pCVR, and never trains pCVR on its own. Instead it trains the product pCTR × pCVR = P(click and convert) against the conversion label over all impressions. pCVR becomes whatever makes that product right everywhere, not just on the clicked subset.

A naive pCVR model (trained on clicks only) is well calibrated on clicked impressions. Why can it still be biased in the auction?

  • The attribution window is too short
  • The auction applies it to all candidates, and non-clicking users and less clickbaity ads convert differently from the clicked population it learned from
  • It has too few conversions
  • pCTR is miscalibrated

Calibrated on the training distribution doesn't mean calibrated on the serving distribution. The mismatch is selection: training saw only clicks. ESMM, or reweighting clicked examples by the inverse of their click probability (IPW again, from Chapter 5), addresses it.

12.5Switch on conversion bidding

Time to change how Pebble sells ads. In the treatment arm, every advertiser bids its target CPA, and your stack computes CPA × pCTR × pCVR. The headline metric for conversion bidding isn't revenue. It's CPA adherence: did advertisers actually pay about their target per conversion? Paying 30% more than the target means the platform broke its promise, and paying much less means it under-delivered.

After launching oCPM, revenue is up 20%, but advertisers' actual CPA is 35% above their targets. What's the most likely model issue, and the risk?

  • pCVR is over-predicted, so bids and prices are inflated. Advertisers overpay per conversion and will cut budgets
  • pCVR is under-predicted
  • pCTR is too low
  • No issue: revenue is up

Under oCPM the platform prices with its own predictions, so over-prediction is literally overcharging. Short-term revenue rises, and advertisers notice at the end of the month. Autobidding systems usually add a feedback controller that adjusts bids per campaign when actual CPA drifts from target. That controller is only as good as the calibration it corrects.

Nimbus (a bank, where conversions take ~5 days) reports that under oCPM they get 40% fewer conversions than their budget should buy, while their actual CPA is well below target. What's happening, and what do you propose?

  • Diagnosis: a CPA below target with under-delivery means pCVR is under-predicted for Nimbus. Their bids are too low, so they lose auctions. Delayed feedback is the prime suspect: with ~5-day delays and daily training, a big share of recent Nimbus clicks are labeled as non-conversions.
  • Evidence to pull: Nimbus's pCVR calibration by label age (recent vs. matured), and the delay distribution of their conversions.
  • Fixes: delay correction or delayed-feedback modeling for slow advertisers; excluding immature negatives; a calibration layer segmented by vertical; and, short-term, the autobidder's CPA controller raising Nimbus's bids.
  • Why it matters: systematic under-delivery for long-cycle verticals is a revenue and fairness problem. Finance and travel advertisers are often among the most valuable.

A conversion model (naive and ESMM), a delay correction using your observedFraction(), and conversion bidding in your auction, judged by whether advertisers pay what they asked to pay.
