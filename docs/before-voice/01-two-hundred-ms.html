Chapter 1 · Part I · The Marketplace

Two Hundred Milliseconds

Every time someone scrolls Pebble, a small auction house opens, runs, and closes before the next photo loads. In this chapter you build the first working version of it.

A user opens Pebble at 9:14 pm and starts scrolling. Three feed slots ahead of them are reserved for ads. About 200 milliseconds from now, those slots need to be filled. The ads have to be eligible, relevant, and correctly priced, and the whole decision has to be written down so the system can learn from it.

This chapter follows that request from start to finish. It also starts the project that runs through the whole book: your own ad stack. It's a small but real ad system that runs in this browser against a simulated Pebble, with 4,000 users, 60 advertisers, and about 340 ads. By the end of the chapter your stack will serve real (simulated) traffic.

1.1The journey of one ad request

Here is what happens between the scroll and the ad, in order. Every production ad system has roughly these stages, though names vary between companies.

  1. Request. The app asks the ad server for ads, sending who the user is and the context: device, time, which surface, how many slots.
  2. Eligibility. Most ads can't be shown to this user right now. The campaign might not be running today, or it targets another country or age group, or its budget is spent, or the user has already seen it too often (a frequency cap). Filtering is cheap, exact, and happens first.
  3. Retrieval (also called candidate generation). Pebble's real ad inventory runs to millions. Retrieval uses cheap models to cut that down to a few hundred or a few thousand plausible candidates. Chapter 10 is about this stage.
  4. Ranking. Models predict, for each candidate, the probability of what the advertiser cares about: a click (pCTR), a conversion (pCVR), or a negative reaction like hiding the ad. Big systems rank in two stages: a light model over many candidates, then a heavy one over the survivors.
  5. Auction. Predictions are combined with bids into a score, the winners are chosen, and each winner's price is set. Chapter 2.
  6. Serve. The winners are returned and rendered in the feed.
  7. Log. Everything about the decision is written down: which ads were candidates, what was predicted, what was shown, and later, what the user did. This is the raw material for every model in the book. Chapter 4.

1.2Why a funnel? The latency budget

If computation were free, you'd score every ad with your best model. It isn't. The ad server has a hard deadline: the feed can't wait. A typical budget gives the ad server something like 100 ms end to end, and ranking gets a fraction of that. A heavy ranking model might cost half a millisecond of compute per candidate. Two thousand candidates at 0.5 ms each is a full second. That's why systems use stages, each with a cheaper model than the next.

The catch is that every stage can only lose good ads, never find them. If retrieval drops the best ad, no ranking model downstream can bring it back. Play with the tradeoff below. The simulation draws 20,000 eligible ads with hidden true values (a real system has far more, but the shape of the tradeoff is the same). Each stage estimates those values with some noise (cheaper models are noisier) and keeps its top K.

The funnel under a deadline
Keep more candidates at each stage and the best ad survives more often, but latency grows. Requests that blow the budget time out and show no ad at all.
Intuition

There's a sweet spot. Too narrow a funnel throws away good ads early. Too wide, and you blow the latency budget: timeouts become blank slots, and blank slots earn nothing. A better model in an early stage often beats a bigger model in the last one, because the late model can only choose among what it's given.

In your job

Almost every ads-ranking proposal is secretly a latency proposal. "Let's use a bigger model" really means "let's spend more milliseconds per candidate." Ask three questions: what's the p99 latency impact (the slowest 1% of requests are the ones that time out), what's the compute cost, and which stage are we improving? A quality win in late ranking can't fix a recall problem in retrieval.

Your retrieval stage keeps 500 of 2 million eligible ads. The late-stage ranking team ships a model that's much better at ordering its 50 inputs. Which problem can this model not fix?

  • Showing the second-best of the 50 finalists instead of the best
  • The best ad for this user was never among the 500 retrieved
  • Prices being set with inaccurate predictions
  • Showing irrelevant ads that made it through retrieval

A downstream stage only sees what upstream stages pass along. Losing a good candidate early is a recall problem, and it's invisible to late-stage metrics: the late ranker's offline numbers look great on the candidates it sees. That's why retrieval teams measure recall against a stronger reference model (Chapter 10).

Your late-stage ranker costs 0.5 ms of compute per candidate, and the ranking budget is 40 ms. Ignoring parallelism and overheads, what's the most candidates it can score?

Hint

Budget ÷ cost per candidate.

40 ms ÷ 0.5 ms = 80 candidates. Real systems batch candidates on GPUs, so cost isn't strictly linear, but the principle holds: the late ranker's candidate count is set by its cost per candidate. Doubling model size roughly halves how many candidates it can see.

In your job

Eligibility looks trivial, and it's where many of the scariest bugs live. An eligibility bug that shows alcohol ads to minors, or runs a campaign past its end date, isn't a quality regression. It's a policy incident, or a billing dispute. Eligibility code is usually owned by a different team from ranking, and it deserves its own tests and monitoring.

1.4Build: your first pCTR, with no model

The auction needs, for every candidate, a prediction of the click-through rate: the probability this user clicks this ad if we show it in the top slot. You don't have a model yet. You don't even have logs; those come in Chapter 4. But you do have last month's dashboard, and some common sense.

Last month's dashboard
Top-slot CTR by ad category, from a month when ads were served at random. This is the prior.catCTR array your function receives.

You also have each user's engagement history: the categories of the 12 organic posts they most recently liked or commented on, most recent first. It's a strong signal. A user who has been engaging with hiking photos is probably more interested in Trailhead's boots right now.

Write a heuristic that starts from the category's CTR and adjusts it using the history. There's no single right answer. The tests require only that you return valid probabilities and that you order ads better than a coin flip, measured against the true click probabilities, which only a simulator can know. See how high you can push that score.

Going deeper: why not just use each ad's own CTR?

An ad's historical CTR is a strong signal, and Chapter 6 turns it into a counting feature. But it has two problems as a whole strategy. New ads have no history (the cold-start problem). And an ad's CTR averages over every user it was shown to, so it can't tell you whether this user will click. Personalization needs signals about the user–ad pair, like the history match in your heuristic.

Common trap

"Our CTR is 3.4%, that's great!" Compared to what? A CTR on its own tells you almost nothing. It depends on ad load, slot position, category mix, and how much traffic went to which users. What matters is the gap to a better system, and in real life you only ever see that gap through an experiment (Chapter 9). The oracle here is a luxury of simulation. It tells us how much headroom better predictions could unlock, and that headroom is the business case for the rest of this book.

In the day simulation, the oracle serves the same requests with perfect click predictions and gets more clicks and more revenue. Where does the extra revenue come from?

  • Advertisers bid more for better predictions
  • Better predictions put ads that users actually want to click in the slots, so more impressions turn into billable clicks
  • The oracle shows more ads
  • The oracle charges higher prices per click

With CPC bidding, the platform is paid per click. The auction ranks by bid × pCTR, so when pCTR is wrong, the auction gives slots to ads that won't be clicked. That's lost revenue for Pebble, wasted attention for users, and missed customers for advertisers. Better prediction is the rare lever that helps all three. (Per-click prices can move either way; Chapter 3 explains why.)

1.6Who owns what

At a real company, the stages you just wired together are owned by different teams, each with its own metrics:

StageTypical ownerTheir headline metricWhat can go wrong silently
Eligibility & targetingAdvertiser products / deliveryDelivery correctnessAds shown outside targeting; campaigns not spending
RetrievalRetrieval / candidate generationRecall@K vs. a referenceGood ads never reach ranking
RankingAds quality / rankingAUC, normalized entropy, calibrationOffline gains that don't move online metrics
AuctionMarketplace / monetizationRevenue, advertiser ROI, price stabilityMiscalibrated predictions becoming mispricing
Logging & dataData infraFreshness, completenessLabel bugs that corrupt every model at once

The retrieval team proposes doubling the number of candidates passed to ranking. Their offline analysis shows recall of the eventual winners goes from 91% to 96%. Infra estimates p99 ad-server latency goes from 140 ms to 175 ms. The budget is 200 ms. What do you want to know before saying yes?

A strong answer covers:

  • Tail behaviour, not averages. What's the p99.9, and what's the timeout rate today versus projected? Timeouts are blank slots and zero revenue. Are the slow requests concentrated in certain markets or older devices?
  • Where does the recall gain turn into value? Recall of winners is good, but what's the expected revenue or advertiser-value gain? Five points of recall on low-value requests is worth less than two points on high-value ones.
  • Cost. Twice the candidates means roughly twice the ranking compute. What does that cost per day, and is there a cheaper way to get the same recall, such as a better retrieval model rather than a wider funnel?
  • Headroom for others. If retrieval spends 35 ms of the budget, what's left for the next ranking-model launch? Latency is a shared budget, and it needs an owner.
  • How will we know? An A/B test measuring revenue, advertiser value, and timeout rate, with latency guardrails.

Key terms

EligibilityHard filters (dates, targeting, budgets, caps) applied before any model.
RetrievalCheap stage that cuts millions of ads down to hundreds of candidates.
pCTRPredicted probability of a click, if the ad is shown.
Latency budgetThe time the ad server has to respond; it caps model size × candidate count.
RecallShare of the ads that should win which survive a stage.
OracleA simulator-only system with perfect predictions; it measures headroom.
