Chapter 4 · Part II · Data

Logging: The Memory of the System

A model can only learn from what was written down, and only if it was written down correctly, at the right moment.

Your stack has been serving ads, but it has no memory. Every request vanished once it was served. Before you can train a single model, you need logs. This is the least glamorous chapter in the book, and possibly the most important. When ML teams say "our model got worse and we don't know why," the cause is very often in the logs.

4.1What gets written down

An ad system writes several separate streams of records, at different times, from different machines:

  • The impression log, written by the ad server at serve time: request id, timestamp, user and context, which ad was shown in which slot, the bid, the prediction the model made, the price, the model version, and a snapshot of the feature values the model used.
  • The click log, written seconds or minutes later when the user taps. It records the request id and ad id, so it can be matched back.
  • The conversion log, written hours or days later, often reported by the advertiser's website or app through a pixel or a server-to-server API. It's frequently delayed, sometimes duplicated, occasionally lost.

Training data is made by joining these streams: each impression becomes one training row, and its label is "was there a matching click (or conversion) inside the allowed window?"

4.2Build: the label join

The join decides what a positive example is. Three rules matter:

  1. Match on the right key. One request shows three ads, so the key is (request id, ad id), not just request id.
  2. Windows. A click counts if it comes within the click window after the impression (say one hour). A conversion counts if it comes within the attribution window (say 7 days) and follows a click.
  3. Now. An event that hasn't happened by the time the join runs doesn't exist yet. Your function gets nowT and must ignore anything later. This is how the real pipeline behaves, and it matters a lot in Chapter 12.
Common trap

Joining on request id alone. With three ads per request, a click on the slot-2 ad would also label slots 1 and 3 as clicked, tripling your "positives" with noise. It's the kind of bug that doesn't crash anything. Metrics just get slightly worse, and nobody knows why for a quarter. Label pipelines deserve unit tests like the ones you just passed.

4.3Windows and "now"

Conversions arrive slowly. For a mobile game, they come in minutes. For a bank account, they can take days. If the training job runs right at the end of day 9, impressions from day 9 have had no time to convert, so they get labeled 0 even though some will convert tomorrow. Intuition

Every label is a statement made at a particular time. "Didn't convert" really means "hadn't converted by the time we looked." Fresh data is the most valuable data for a model, because it's closest to today's world. It's also the data with the most wrong labels. Chapter 12 is about living with that tradeoff.

Your pCVR model trains every morning on the last 14 days of impressions, joined at 6 am. Nimbus (a bank, where conversions take days) complains that the model systematically under-predicts its conversions. Gaming advertisers don't see this. What's the most likely cause?

  • Nimbus's creatives are low quality
  • Recent Nimbus impressions haven't had time to convert when the join runs, so they're labeled as negatives
  • The click window is too short
  • Feature hashing collisions

With slow conversions, a big share of the last few days' eventual conversions haven't arrived when the join runs. Those rows become false negatives, and the model learns that Nimbus converts less than it really does. Gaming conversions arrive within hours, so they're barely affected. This is delayed feedback. Fixes include waiting longer before using data (at the cost of freshness), weighting, or modeling the delay explicitly (Chapter 12).

4.4Log features at serve time

Why store feature values in the impression log at all, rather than recomputing them at training time from the raw data? Because features change. Take your stack's counting feature from Chapter 6: an ad's historical CTR. At serve time on day 3, the model saw that ad's CTR as of day 3. If you recompute the feature at training time on day 10, you get the CTR as of day 10, which includes clicks that happened after the impression. That's information from the future.

Training on recomputed features creates train/serve skew: the model learns from feature values it will never see in production. Worse, because the recomputed values include the outcome period, they leak the label. The model looks great offline and disappoints online. The widget compares the two for your logs.

In your job

Serve-time feature logging is expensive: it multiplies log volume. There's steady pressure to cut it ("we can just recompute"). Push back with the skew argument, or at least insist on measuring the skew before switching. The same logic applies to what else you log: the model version (so you can attribute changes), the candidates that lost (so you can study what you didn't show), and exploration flags and probabilities (Chapter 5). Data you didn't log is data you can never learn from.

4.5Downsampling, and undoing it

Real impression logs are enormous, and most of the rows are negatives (no click). Training pipelines usually keep every positive but only a fraction \(w\) of negatives. That's cheaper, and it barely hurts ranking. But a model trained on downsampled data predicts on the downsampled scale: its probabilities are too high. You undo it with a correction that every ads engineer knows, and that every ads PM should recognize when it shows up in a design doc:

\[ q = \frac{p}{p + (1-p)/w} \]

where \(p\) is the model's raw prediction and \(q\) is the corrected probability on the real traffic.

Your pCTR model was trained keeping all clicks but only 10% of non-clicks (\(w = 0.1\)). For some impression it outputs \(p = 0.2\). What's the calibrated CTR \(q\)? (Answer as a probability, e.g. 0.05.)

Hint

(1 − p)/w = 0.8 / 0.1 = 8.

q = 0.2 / (0.2 + 8) ≈ 0.0244, or 2.4%. Forgetting this correction makes a model look 8× over-calibrated. It's a surprisingly common launch bug, and it's why calibration is checked on unsampled evaluation data.

Which of these does not need to be in the impression log for your stack to learn well?

  • The pCTR the model predicted at serve time
  • The slot the ad was shown in
  • The user's click, if any
  • The feature values used by the model

The click hasn't happened yet at serve time. It lives in a separate event stream and gets joined later. Everything else on the list must be captured at serve time, because it can't be reconstructed faithfully afterwards.

Infra proposes cutting storage costs 40% by (a) no longer logging feature values and recomputing them at training time, and (b) keeping only 5% of non-click impressions. How do you evaluate the proposal?

  • (a) is risky, and it's measurable. Ask for a skew analysis: for a sample of logged impressions, compare logged and recomputed values feature by feature. Which features drift (counting features, anything time-windowed, anything mutable like account status)? Then train on recomputed features and evaluate on logged ones. The AUC gap is your skew cost. A middle ground: log only the features that can't be faithfully recomputed.
  • (b) is usually fine for ranking, if done right. Check that the correction \(q = p/(p+(1-p)/w)\) is applied in serving, that evaluation uses unsampled data, and that rare segments (new advertisers, small countries) keep enough negatives to be calibrated. Consider segment-aware sampling rates.
  • Don't lose the ability to learn later. Keep a small unsampled, fully logged holdout stream for audits, research, and future models.
  • Put numbers on both sides: the storage saving vs. the expected metric loss converted to revenue.
