Chapter 10 · Part IV · The Models

Retrieval

Before you can rank the best ads, you have to find them. Out of millions of ads, in a few milliseconds, with a model that has never seen the user and the ad together.

Your stack scores every eligible ad for every request: about 150 of them. That works because Pebble's simulated inventory is small. Real inventory is millions of ads, and the logistic regression you shipped in Chapter 9 costs microseconds per candidate. Chapter 1's funnel is about to become real for you. In this chapter you'll build the stage that picks a short list: a two-tower retrieval model, the workhorse of modern recommendation and ads retrieval.

10.1Two towers and a dot product

The constraint that shapes retrieval is simple: you can't run a model over every (user, ad) pair per request. The two-tower trick is to split the model so that the user side and the ad side never meet until the very last step:

  • A user tower turns everything about the user and context into a vector \(u\): history, demographics, and so on.
  • An ad tower turns everything about the ad into a vector \(a\): its ID, category, and advertiser.
  • The score is their dot product, \(s = u \cdot a\).

Because the ad tower never looks at the user, every ad's vector can be computed ahead of time and stored in an index. At request time you compute one user vector and ask the index for the ads with the largest dot products. That's a maximum inner product search, which approximate nearest neighbour (ANN) libraries like FAISS, ScaNN, and HNSW answer over millions of vectors in a millisecond or two.

Why can't a two-tower retrieval model use the feature "user's past CTR on this advertiser"?

  • It's leaky
  • It depends on both the user and the ad, so it can't be computed in either tower alone, and the ad vectors couldn't be precomputed
  • It's too big to store
  • Dot products can't use numeric features

Every input must belong to exactly one tower. A (user, ad) pair feature belongs to neither. This is the fundamental accuracy-for-speed trade of two-tower models, and why retrieval is followed by a ranking model that can look at the pair.

10.2Training a retrieval model

A ranking model learns from impressions: shown ads, clicked or not. A retrieval model has a different job. It has to push good ads above everything else in the corpus, including millions of ads nobody ever showed this user. So retrieval is usually trained with a sampled softmax: for each click, the clicked ad competes against a handful of randomly sampled ads (negatives), and the model learns to give the clicked one the highest score:

\[ \mathcal{L} = -\log \frac{e^{u \cdot a_{\text{clicked}}}}{e^{u \cdot a_{\text{clicked}}} + \sum_{j=1}^{k} e^{u \cdot a_{\text{neg},j}}} \]

Which negatives you sample matters enormously. Random negatives teach coarse distinctions (a gaming fan doesn't want a mortgage ad). "Hard" negatives, meaning ads that were shown but not clicked, teach fine ones. Sampling in proportion to popularity requires a correction (the "logQ correction," Yi et al., 2019), or the model learns to penalize popular ads.

10.3Build: top-K by dot product

Your stack's retrieval step: given a user vector and the ad vectors, return the K best. Production systems use approximate indexes; yours does it exactly, which is fine for 340 ads.

For one request, the 10 ads with the highest true value are known. Retrieval returned 30 candidates, and 6 of those 10 are among them. What's recall@30?

Hint

Recall = (relevant items retrieved) / (relevant items).

6 / 10 = 0.6. The four missing ads can never be shown, however good the ranker is (Chapter 1). Retrieval is judged by recall, not precision: the ranker will sort out the other 24.

Intuition

Your logged clicks only exist for ads your old system showed. A retrieval model trained on them learns a blurry copy of the old system's taste, including its blind spots. The same architecture trained on unbiased data does far better. That gap is the price of exposure bias (Chapter 5), and it's why real retrieval systems train on broader signals than their own ad clicks: organic engagement, exploration traffic, and every surface where the user expresses interest.

In your job

Retrieval work rarely shows up as a big number on its own. Its value is headroom: a cheaper candidate set frees latency for a bigger ranker, and better recall lets every ranker improvement land. When a retrieval team asks for resources, ask how they measure recall (against what reference?), how often ad embeddings are refreshed (new ads can't be retrieved until they're indexed), and which sources the final candidate set mixes. Most systems union several retrievers: two-tower, heuristics, recency, and advertiser-specific.

A new advertiser complains that their ads get almost no impressions in the first 48 hours, even with high bids. Using what you know about two-tower retrieval, list the likely causes and the fixes you'd ask about.

  • Not indexed yet. Ad vectors are computed in batch. If the index refreshes daily, a new ad can't be retrieved until the next build. Ask for incremental indexing.
  • Cold embedding. The ad-ID embedding is untrained (near zero) for a new ad, so its vector relies on category and advertiser embeddings. If the advertiser is also new, there's almost nothing. Ask whether the ad tower uses content features (text, image embeddings) that exist from day one.
  • Bids don't reach retrieval. Retrieval scores by predicted relevance. High bids only matter once the ad reaches the auction. Ask whether a bid-aware or value-aware retrieval source exists.
  • Exposure bias. No impressions means no clicks, which means no training signal. Ask about a dedicated new-ad retrieval source or exploration quota (Chapter 5), with a measured cost.
