Chapter 18 · Extra Credit

When the Model Remembers

In which I remember your running shoes just as you start looking for a tent.

Okay, Pebble knows you love running. Months of shoe reviews, race routes, and training plans. Then this morning you start looking at campsites. Do we show you another pair of trainers, or the lantern you suddenly seem to need?

That one request contains two stories. Pebble's older history says this person keeps coming back to running; the actions arriving this morning say they may be planning a trip. We want the second story to reach the next ad request quickly without deciding that a single morning has erased the first. Here long-term means older user behavior, a different question from the long-term user outcomes in Chapter 15.

We'll follow those histories through candidate generation and into the auction. The four diagrams are illustrations, not models trained on your Pebble logs. Pebble keeps only twelve items of history and its catalog is small enough to search exactly, so it can't honestly show what industrial sequence models gain or what they cost to serve. Everything below is about mechanism.

18.1Two time horizons, one person

A behavior sequence is an ordered list of actions with their times attached. Imagine this user's older history: a race plan saved in April, trail shoes viewed in June, running routes revisited in August. That's different from opening three shoe posts during one bored afternoon, even if a simple category count calls both histories “three running actions.” You could collapse the older actions into one slowly updated user vector, or let a larger model reach back to particular events when it scores an ad. The second option can preserve more detail, but reading thousands of events on every request costs time. Our first diagram uses the compressed version so you can see the two time horizons separately.

Now look at the live session. At 9:02 the user opens a campsite page; at 9:04 they save a campsite; at 9:06 they compare tents. A user vector built overnight knows about the running but misses this morning entirely. If the new actions reach serving before the next request, a sequence model can read them in order and give the tent search more weight. Order can matter: shoes → campsite and campsite → shoes contain the same two categories, but may point to different immediate interests.

Let's put numbers on that tension. Our long-term summary says “80% running, 20% camping,” while the live score starts at “60% running, 40% camping” after the first campsite view. These are toy preference scores, not observed click probabilities. Each additional camping action moves the live score halfway toward camping; the combined score gives 40% weight to the older summary and 60% to the live one. The dials are doing, by hand, what a real model learns: how much weight each horizon deserves, and for which candidate. A running-shoe ad may still benefit from the months of older actions, while a tent ad may depend more on what happened ten minutes ago. You can update the recent-action input as events arrive while keeping the older representation on a slower schedule. Pinterest described a combination of recent actions and batch-built user embeddings in TransAct; its later TransAct V2 explores much longer action sequences.

Common trap

“More history” is not automatically better. A long sequence can be expensive to read, noisy, and full of things you stopped caring about. Ask which events count, how old they can be, how the sequence is compressed, and whether the improvement survives a latency and cost budget.

18.2Two ways to propose a candidate

Chapter 10's two-tower retriever puts a user vector near ad vectors and searches an index for neighbors. Another approach predicts an item's identifier, token by token, from the action sequence. In TIGER, those identifiers are learned semantic codes: generating (4, 2, 7) means looking up an item with that code. It does not mean writing a new ad or generating prose.

The same request, two candidate sources
Step through a conceptual two-tower path and a semantic-ID decoding path. Their outputs are candidate IDs, not auction winners.

Why consider the second path? Sequential decoding can use the order of actions when proposing candidates, while nearest-neighbor search depends on what its embeddings and index represent. Whether that helps in ads is an empirical question. Compare recall against an appropriate reference, coverage of new ads, candidate diversity, latency, and compute cost on the same traffic. A hybrid may beat either source alone; it also costs more to serve.

18.3A generated ID is still just a suggestion

Here is where an ads system gets less romantic. An ID might refer to an ad that has been removed. A valid ad may target a different audience, have an exhausted budget, or fail a policy check. The auction can only rank candidates that make it through the gates. Watch six illustrative IDs get whittled down.

For ads, this is a first-class serving problem, not a detail to bolt on after a retrieval paper. A 2026 paper on serving generative ads retrieval, GRACE, reports that filtering by targeting during decoding raised the share of generated candidates that were actually eligible from about 24% to 40%. In other words, even with that fix, most of what the generator proposed couldn't be shown to that user. The PM question is simple: how many eligible, useful ads do we get per millisecond and per dollar of serving cost? Measure that before celebrating a better offline candidate score.

18.4“Real time” has four clocks

What does “real time” mean in that live session? Suppose another campsite action happens at 10:02, right as an advertiser launches a lantern ad. Recording the action, making it available to the model at serving time, adding the lantern to the retrieval index, and updating model weights are four separate steps. Press Play to follow one hypothetical schedule for them.

What is fresh, and when?
Hypothetical clock times to separate four mechanisms. Your actual system might use very different cadences.

By 10:02:02 in this example, the next request can include the campsite action even though the model's weights are still yesterday's (Chapter 14's retraining schedule hasn't run yet). The input changed, so its prediction can change; it depends on whether training taught the model to use recent actions. The new lantern has a separate problem: until the ad index refreshes, retrieval cannot propose it at all. When weights eventually update, they incorporate newly available training examples rather than somehow making earlier requests retroactively fresh.

That gives you a more useful debugging path. If the latest action is missing, inspect event delivery and online features. If the new ad never appears, inspect indexing and eligibility. If the model sees the new action but keeps preferring yesterday's pattern, look at what its training taught it and when those weights were last updated. “The model is stale” is a tempting diagnosis. It is also a fairly efficient way to page the wrong team.

In your job

For a proposal to ship a long-sequence or generative retriever, ask for the baseline, eligible recall at a fixed candidate count, serving latency and cost, slice results for new ads and changing interests, and an online experiment. Keep the auction, calibration, budget delivery, and user-quality metrics in view. A better candidate generator does not get a free pass through the rest of the stack.

18.5What would you ship?

For Pebble, I would start with a small, fresh recent-action signal alongside the existing long-term features, measure where it changes candidates, and check that the extra serving work pays for itself. Generative retrieval would earn an experiment when the catalog and behavior histories are large enough for the existing retrievers to leave meaningful recall on the table.

The running shoes should still be somewhere in the model's memory when the campsite search arrives. The interesting question is whether that new signal earns the lantern a place among the candidates. If it does, the lantern still has to clear eligibility and justify the extra milliseconds before it gets anywhere near the auction. That's the end of the extra credit. Go get yourself a lantern.

A user's actions switch from running to camping at 9 am, but the ads they're shown still skew toward running gear an hour later. What do you check first?

  • Retrain the model on more recent data
  • Whether the new actions reached the online features the model reads at serving time
  • Extend the long-term history the model reads
  • Refresh the ad index

Start with the cheapest clock. If this morning's actions never reached serving, no model, however fresh, can react to them. If they did arrive and the ads still don't change, then look at what training taught the model about recent actions, and only then at when the weights last updated.

A generative retriever and a two-tower retriever each return 200 candidates per request. After eligibility checks, 60 of the generative candidates survive and 150 of the two-tower ones do. What should decide between them?

  • The raw number of candidates each returns
  • Eligible, useful candidates per millisecond and per dollar, with recall measured against the same reference
  • The generative model's offline recall on its own
  • Which architecture is more recent

A candidate that fails targeting, budget, or policy costs serving time and delivers nothing. Compare what survives the gates, what it costs to produce, and whether the survivors are the ones worth ranking. A hybrid that unions both sources can win, and it also costs more to run.

Your ranking team proposes extending each user's history from the last 50 actions to the last 5,000. Draft the questions you'd bring to the review.

  • Which actions, and how old? Clicks, saves, views, purchases? A three-year-old action may be noise. Ask how the sequence is filtered and weighted by age.
  • Read or compress? Reading 5,000 events on every request is a latency and cost decision; a compressed summary refreshed offline is cheaper and loses detail. Ask for the serving cost per 1,000 requests of each.
  • Who benefits? Heavy users with long histories should gain; new users gain nothing. Ask for slices by history length, and by users whose interests recently changed.
  • Does fresh still win? A longer memory can drown out this morning's signal. Ask how recent actions reach serving and how they're weighted against the long history.
  • Online proof: an A/B test with latency, revenue, advertiser value, and user-quality guardrails, since a retrieval or ranking gain has to survive the auction and the user.

What you learned

A user carries two stories at once, an older one and a live one, and a good system keeps both without letting either erase the other. Generative retrieval proposes candidates by decoding item codes from those stories, and in ads every proposal still has to survive eligibility before it's worth a millisecond. And "real time" is four separate clocks, which tells you which team to page when something looks stale.

Key terms

Behavior sequenceAn ordered, timestamped list of a user's actions.
Long-term vs. live signalA slowly refreshed summary of older behavior, next to actions from the current session.
Semantic IDA learned code for an item, which a generative retriever decodes token by token.
Generative retrievalProposing candidates by generating item codes from a user's history, instead of nearest-neighbor search.
Eligibility filteringRemoving candidates that fail targeting, budget, or policy before ranking.
Four clocks of freshnessEvent delivery, online features, index refresh, and weight updates, each on its own schedule.
