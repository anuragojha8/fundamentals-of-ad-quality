Chapter 18 · Extra Credit

When the Model Remembers

In which I remember your running shoes just as you start looking for a tent.

Okay, Pebble knows you love running. Months of shoe reviews, race routes, and training plans. Then this morning you start looking at campsites. Do we show you another pair of trainers, or the lantern you suddenly seem to need?

That one request contains two stories. Pebble's older history says this person keeps coming back to running; the actions arriving this morning say they may be planning a trip. We want the second story to reach the next ad request quickly without deciding that a single morning has erased the first. Here long-term means older user behavior, a different question from the long-term user outcomes in Chapter 15.

We'll follow those histories through candidate generation and into the auction. The four diagrams are illustrative mechanisms, not a new model trained on your Pebble logs. Pebble's actual history is only twelve items long, and its small ad catalog is searched exactly. It cannot honestly demonstrate the gains or serving costs of industrial sequence models.

18.1Two time horizons, one person

A behavior sequence is an ordered list of actions with their times attached. Imagine this user's older history: a race plan saved in April, trail shoes viewed in June, running routes revisited in August. That's different from opening three shoe posts during one bored afternoon, even if a simple category count calls both histories “three running actions.” You could collapse the older actions into one slowly updated user vector, or let a larger model reach back to particular events when it scores an ad. The second option can preserve more detail, but reading thousands of events on every request costs time. Our first diagram uses the compressed version so you can see the two time horizons separately.

Now look at the live session. At 9:02 the user opens a campsite page; at 9:04 they save a campsite; at 9:06 they compare tents. A user vector built overnight knows about the running but misses this morning entirely. If the new actions reach serving before the next request, a sequence model can read them in order and give the tent search more weight. Order can matter: shoes → campsite and campsite → shoes contain the same two categories, but may point to different immediate interests. Neither sequence proves the user wants to buy anything.

Let's put numbers on that tension. Our long-term summary says “80% running, 20% camping,” while the live score starts at “60% running, 40% camping” after the first campsite view. These are toy preference scores, not observed click probabilities. Each additional camping action moves the live score halfway toward camping; the combined score gives 40% weight to the older summary and 60% to the live one. The 40/60 blend is something I picked to make the crossing point visible; a real model would learn how to use these signals for a particular candidate. A running-shoe ad may still benefit from the months of older actions, while a tent ad may depend more on what happened ten minutes ago. You can update the recent-action input as events arrive while keeping the older representation on a slower schedule. Pinterest described a combination of recent actions and batch-built user embeddings in TransAct; its later TransAct V2 explores much longer action sequences. These are recommendation examples, not claims that Pebble uses their architecture.

Common trap

“More history” is not automatically better. A long sequence can be expensive to read, noisy, and full of things you stopped caring about. Ask which events count, how old they can be, how the sequence is compressed, and whether the improvement survives a latency and cost budget.

18.2Two ways to propose a candidate

Chapter 10's two-tower retriever puts a user vector near ad vectors and searches an index for neighbors. Another approach predicts an item's identifier, token by token, from the action sequence. In TIGER, those identifiers are learned semantic codes: generating (4, 2, 7) means looking up an item with that code. It does not mean writing a new ad or generating prose.

The same request, two candidate sources
Step through a conceptual two-tower path and a semantic-ID decoding path. Their outputs are candidate IDs, not auction winners.

Why consider the second path? Sequential decoding can use the order of actions when proposing candidates, while nearest-neighbor search depends on what its embeddings and index represent. Whether that helps in ads is an empirical question. Compare recall against an appropriate reference, coverage of new ads, candidate diversity, latency, and compute cost on the same traffic. A hybrid may beat either source alone; it also costs more to serve.

18.3A generated ID is still just a suggestion

Here is where an ads system gets less romantic. An ID might refer to an ad that has been removed. A valid ad may target a different audience, have an exhausted budget, or fail a policy check. The auction can only rank candidates that make it through the gates. Watch six illustrative IDs get whittled down.

For ads, this is a first-class serving problem, not a detail to bolt on after a retrieval paper. Recent research on generative ads retrieval explicitly studies eligibility filtering and decoding cost. The PM question is simple: how many eligible, useful ads do we get per millisecond and per dollar of serving cost? Measure that before celebrating a better offline candidate score.

18.4“Real time” has four clocks

What does “real time” mean in that live session? Suppose another campsite action happens at 10:02, right as an advertiser launches a lantern ad. Recording the action, making it available to the model at serving time, adding the lantern to the retrieval index, and updating model weights are four separate steps. What is fresh, and when?
Hypothetical clock times to separate four mechanisms. Your actual system might use very different cadences.

By 10:02:02 in this example, the next request can include the campsite action even though the model's weights are still yesterday's. The input changed, so its prediction can change; it depends on whether training taught the model to use recent actions. The new lantern has a separate problem: until the ad index refreshes, retrieval cannot propose it at all. When weights eventually update, they incorporate newly available training examples rather than somehow making earlier requests retroactively fresh.

That gives you a more useful debugging path. If the latest action is missing, inspect event delivery and online features. If the new ad never appears, inspect indexing and eligibility. If the model sees the new action but keeps preferring yesterday's pattern, look at what its training taught it and when those weights were last updated. “The model is stale” is a tempting diagnosis. It is also a fairly efficient way to page the wrong team.

In your job

For a proposal to ship a long-sequence or generative retriever, ask for the baseline, eligible recall at a fixed candidate count, serving latency and cost, slice results for new ads and changing interests, and an online experiment. Keep the auction, calibration, budget delivery, and user-quality metrics in view. A better candidate generator does not get a free pass through the rest of the stack.

18.5What would you ship?

For Pebble, I would start with a small, fresh recent-action signal alongside the existing long-term features, measure where it changes candidates, and check that the extra serving work pays for itself. Generative retrieval would earn an experiment when the catalog and behavior histories are large enough for the existing retrievers to leave meaningful recall on the table. That is a roadmap hypothesis, not a result from our toy world.

The running shoes should still be somewhere in the model's memory when the campsite search arrives. The interesting question is whether that new signal earns the lantern a place among the candidates. If it does, the lantern still has to clear eligibility and justify the extra milliseconds before it gets anywhere near the auction. See ya back there.
