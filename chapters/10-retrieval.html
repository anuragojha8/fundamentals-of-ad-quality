10.1Two towers and a dot product

Here's the constraint that shapes everything about retrieval: you cannot run a model over every (user, ad) pair on every request. You just can't. So how would you design around that? Give it a second.

The two-tower trick is beautifully simple: split the model so the user side and the ad side never meet until the very last step.

  • A user tower turns everything about the user and context into a vector \(u\): history, demographics, and so on.
  • An ad tower turns everything about the ad into a vector \(a\): its ID, category, and advertiser.
  • The score is just their dot product, \(s = u \cdot a\).

Here's why that's so clever. Since the ad tower never looks at the user, you can compute every ad's vector ahead of time and stash them all in an index. Then at request time, you compute one user vector and ask the index, "which ads have the biggest dot product with this?" That's called maximum inner product search, and approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) indexes (FAISS, ScaNN, or graph-based HNSW) can answer it over millions of vectors in a millisecond or two. Pretty neat, right?

Why can't a two-tower retrieval model use the feature "user's past CTR on this advertiser"?

  • It's leaky
  • It depends on both the user and the ad, so it can't be computed in either tower alone, and the ad vectors couldn't be precomputed
  • It's too big to store
  • Dot products can't use numeric features

Every input has to live in exactly one tower, and a (user, ad) pair feature doesn't belong to either. That's the fundamental accuracy-for-speed deal you make with two-tower models, and it's why retrieval is always followed by a ranking model that can look at the pair.

10.2Training a retrieval model

A ranking model learns from impressions: ads that were shown, clicked or not. A retrieval model has a very different job. It has to push good ads above everything else in the corpus, including the millions of ads nobody ever showed this user.

So retrieval models usually get trained with a sampled softmax. For each click, the clicked ad goes up against a handful of randomly sampled ads (called negatives), and the model learns to give the clicked one the highest score. Like a lineup at a police station, except the suspect is an ad for hiking boots:

\[ \mathcal{L} = -\log \frac{e^{u \cdot a_{\text{clicked}}}}{e^{u \cdot a_{\text{clicked}}} + \sum_{j=1}^{k} e^{u \cdot a_{\text{neg},j}}} \]

Which negatives you pick matters a lot. Random negatives teach coarse lessons (a gaming fan does not want a mortgage ad). "Hard" negatives, ads that were shown but not clicked, teach subtle ones. And if you sample negatives in proportion to popularity, you need a correction (the "logQ correction," Yi et al., 2019), or the model learns to punish popular ads just for being popular.

10.3Build: top-K by dot product

Your stack's retrieval step: given a user vector and all the ad vectors, return the K best. Real production systems use approximate indexes for speed. Yours does it exactly, which is totally fine for 340 ads.

For one request, the 10 ads with the highest true value are known. Retrieval returned 30 candidates, and 6 of those 10 are among them. What's recall@30?

Hint

Recall = (relevant items retrieved) / (relevant items).

6 / 10 = 0.6. Those four missing ads can never be shown, no matter how brilliant the ranker is (hello again, Chapter 1). That's why retrieval gets judged on recall, not precision. The ranker will sort out the other 24.

Intuition

Did your hand-written heuristic just beat your fancy neural network? Don't feel bad. It's supposed to. Your logged clicks only exist for ads your old system chose to show, so a retrieval model trained on them learns a blurry copy of the old system's taste, blind spots included. Train the exact same architecture on unbiased data and it does way better. That gap is the price of exposure bias (Chapter 5, back to haunt us), and it's why real retrieval systems train on much broader signals than their own ad clicks: organic engagement, exploration traffic, and anywhere else the user tells you what they're into.

In your job

Retrieval work rarely shows up as a big flashy number on its own. Its value is headroom: a cheaper candidate set frees up latency for a bigger ranker, and better recall lets every future ranker improvement actually land. So when a retrieval team asks for resources, ask how they measure recall (against what reference?), how often ad embeddings get refreshed (new ads can't be retrieved until they're indexed), and which sources go into the final candidate set. Most real systems union several retrievers together: two-tower, heuristics, recency, and advertiser-specific ones.

A new advertiser complains that their ads get almost no impressions in the first 48 hours, even with high bids. Using what you know about two-tower retrieval, list the likely causes and the fixes you'd ask about.

  • Not indexed yet. Ad vectors get computed in batches. If the index only refreshes daily, a new ad literally can't be retrieved until the next build. Ask for incremental indexing.
  • Cold embedding. The ad-ID embedding for a brand-new ad is untrained (basically zero), so its vector leans on category and advertiser embeddings. If the advertiser is also new, there's almost nothing to go on. Ask whether the ad tower uses content features (text, image embeddings) that exist from day one.
  • Bids don't reach retrieval. Retrieval scores by predicted relevance. High bids only matter once the ad makes it to the auction. Ask whether there's a bid-aware or value-aware retrieval source.
  • Exposure bias. No impressions means no clicks, which means no training signal, which means no impressions. Ask about a dedicated new-ad retrieval source or exploration quota (Chapter 5), with a measured cost.

What you built

A two-tower retrieval model trained with sampled softmax, your own top-K search, and a real funnel in production: retrieval → your ranker → your auction. Chapter 1 would be proud.

Your two-tower is fast precisely because it keeps users and ads apart until the very end. But your ranker doesn't have that constraint. It only sees a few dozen candidates, so it can afford to let users and ads interact as much as it likes. Next time, we build a ranker that takes full advantage of that. Key terms

Two-tower modelSeparate user and ad networks; score = dot product.
ANN indexApproximate nearest-neighbor search over precomputed ad vectors.
Sampled softmaxThe clicked ad vs. k sampled negatives; the standard retrieval loss.
NegativesRandom, hard (shown but not clicked), or in-batch; they shape what's learned.
Recall@KShare of truly best items that make the top K.
Exposure biasTraining only on what was shown caps what retrieval can learn.
