Chapter 3 · Part I · The Marketplace

Predictions Are Prices

In which my model overcharges Lumen, and the only one who notices is Lumen.

Remember that innocent little line you wrote in Chapter 2? price = next.score / c.pctr. I need you to look at it again. That division sign is where machine learning quietly turns into money.

This chapter is about what happens when c.pctr is wrong. Which, I'm sorry to tell you, is always. By some amount. For every single ad.

3.1Two ways to be wrong about an ad

Let's say the true CTR of Trailhead's ad is 3%, but our model, full of confidence, says 6%. Two things happen in the auction, and they pull in opposite directions:

  1. Trailhead wins more. Its score (bid × pCTR) just doubled, so it steals slots from ads that users would actually have clicked more.
  2. Trailhead pays less per click. The price divides by pCTR, so doubling pCTR halves the per-click price for the same slot.

So Pebble loses twice: it shows a worse ad and charges it less. Great job, model. Now flip it around. If the model under-predicts Trailhead, Trailhead loses auctions it deserved to win, and when it does manage to win, it pays more per click than is fair. The model's mistake has turned into an overcharge on one specific advertiser's invoice. Nobody typed a wrong number. The math did it.

Let's watch it happen on real traffic. We'll start from perfect predictions (the simulator's truth), then make the model wrong about exactly one advertiser. Everyone else stays perfect.

Lumen's ad has a true CTR of 4%, but the model predicts 2%. Lumen wins the slot, and the runner-up's score (bid × pCTR) is 0.03. What does Lumen pay per click under GSP?

Hint

The auction only knows the prediction: price = runner-up score ÷ Lumen's predicted CTR.

0.03 / 0.02 = $1.50 per click. With a correct prediction, Lumen would've paid 0.03 / 0.04 = $0.75. The model's 2× under-prediction is a 2× overcharge. Here's the part that stings. The auction thinks it's charging Lumen 3 cents per impression, because 1.50 × 0.02 = 0.03, which is exactly the runner-up's score. That's the promise GSP makes: you pay, per impression, what the next ad would have been worth, as measured by the model. But Lumen's ad really does get clicked 4% of the time, so Lumen's actual cost per impression is 0.04 × 1.50 = 6 cents. Double the promise. The measuring stick was wrong, so the bill is wrong. (Holding the runner-up and the slot fixed. A big enough prediction error changes who wins, and then you have to rerun the auction rather than rescale the price.)

3.2Calibration

A model is calibrated if its predictions mean what they say. When it says 2%, then out of all the impressions where it said 2%, about 2% actually got clicked. No more, no less. If we want to be more formal about it:

\[ \mathbb{E}[\,y \mid \hat{p} = p\,] = p \quad \text{for every } p \]

The simplest summary is the calibration ratio: total predicted clicks divided by total actual clicks, \( \sum \hat{p}_i / \sum y_i \). A value of 1.0 is perfect. 1.2 means the model over-predicts by 20%, and 0.8 means it under-predicts by 20%.

But here's the sneaky part: a ratio of 1.0 overall can hide big errors in both directions that happen to cancel out. So we also draw a reliability diagram. Sort predictions into bins, then plot each bin's average prediction against its actual click rate. A calibrated model sits right on the diagonal.

Intuition

Ranking and calibration are two different skills. Ranking asks: does the model put better ads above worse ones? Calibration asks: are the actual numbers right? Multiply every prediction by 2 and the ranking doesn't change at all, while the calibration is completely ruined. That's why Chapter 9 measures them separately: AUC for ranking, and calibration ratio and normalized entropy for the numbers.

3.3When does calibration matter?

Okay, here's a subtle point that trips up even people who've been doing this for years. Suppose every pCTR is off by the same factor. A pure CPC GSP auction… doesn't care. All the scores scale together, so the ranking is the same. And prices divide one prediction by another, so the factor cancels out.

So why does everyone obsess over calibration? Because real systems break those tidy conditions all the time:

  • Errors aren't uniform. The model is too high for some advertisers and too low for others, like in the widget above. Every uneven error is money moving between advertisers, or from Pebble to an advertiser.
  • Mixed bid types. A CPM bid's score doesn't involve pCTR at all. So if pCTR is 20% too high across the board, every CPC ad gets a 20% boost against every CPM ad.
  • Reserve prices are in eCPM, so they compare a predicted value against a fixed number.
  • Conversion bidding multiplies pCTR × pCVR. The errors compound, and the advertiser's cost per conversion depends directly on both being right (Chapter 12).
  • Ads vs. organic content. Deciding whether a slot should show an ad or your friend's beach photo means comparing the ad's predicted value with the post's. Inflated pCTR means too many ads (Chapter 15).
  • Forecasting and budgets. Delivery forecasts, pacing, and reach estimates all take predictions at face value.

A new pCTR model is 15% over-calibrated on every impression, uniformly. All advertisers use CPC bids and there's no reserve. What happens to the auction?

  • Every advertiser pays 15% less per click
  • Rankings change in favor of high-CTR ads
  • Nothing changes: rankings and per-click prices are the same
  • Pebble earns 15% more

All scores scale by 1.15, so the order stays the same. Prices are (1.15 × next score) / (1.15 × own pCTR), so the factor cancels. But this is only true in that idealized, too-good-to-be-true setup: no CPM bids, no reserve, no conversion bidding, no ad-vs-organic decisions, and a perfectly uniform error. The next question takes away one of those conditions. Brace yourself.

Same model, 15% over-calibrated uniformly, but now half of Pebble's advertisers buy on CPM (a fixed price per impression, no pCTR involved). What happens?

  • Still nothing: the error is uniform
  • CPC advertisers win more auctions against CPM advertisers, and the CPM advertisers lose delivery
  • CPM advertisers win more auctions
  • Only the CPC advertisers' prices change

CPC scores get inflated by 15% while CPM scores don't, so CPC ads win impressions that CPM ads would've won on true value. Delivery shifts, prices shift, and CPM advertisers watch their campaigns under-deliver for no reason they can possibly see. That's why calibration gets monitored per bid type and per segment, not just in aggregate.

3.4Build: a calibration layer

So how do we fix a miscalibrated model without retraining the whole thing? Almost every production ranking model has a little helper for exactly this: a calibration layer. It's a small, separate function that runs after the model, gets fitted to recent outcomes, and maps raw predictions to calibrated probabilities. It's cheap, it can be refitted every hour, and it soaks up slow drift between full retrains. The usual suspects:

  • Histogram binning: bucket the predictions and replace each one with its bucket's observed rate. This is the one you'll build.
  • Platt scaling: fit \( \sigma(a \cdot \text{logit}(\hat p) + b) \). Just two parameters.
  • Isotonic regression: the best monotone step function you can fit.

Production systems often calibrate per segment (per country, surface, or ad type), because miscalibration is almost never uniform.

A strictly increasing calibration map preserves the model's ordering. Histogram binning ties predictions in the same bucket, and noisy bucket rates can even reverse neighboring buckets unless constrained. Check ranking after fitting it, not just calibration.

Now fit your calibrator to one day of your heuristic's top-slot traffic, apply it to the next day, and check it against the truth. We only use top-slot impressions because your pCTR is a prediction for the top slot. (The other slots have their own drama, and Chapter 5 deals with it properly.)

Common trap

Calibrating on the same data you evaluate on. It'll look perfect every time, and that should make you suspicious, not happy. Fit on one period and check on the next, the way it'll actually run in production. Also, keep an eye on bins with only a few impressions. A bin with 40 impressions and 3 clicks confidently says the rate is "7.5%," but honestly, who knows. That's why the reference solution nudges each bin a little toward its average prediction.

In your job

Treat calibration like a pricing SLA, because that's what it is. Good ads teams have dashboards of calibration ratio by advertiser, by vertical, by country, by bid type, and by new vs. established ads, with alerts whenever a slice drifts outside a band (say ±10%). When an advertiser escalates about costs, the very first chart to pull is their predicted vs. actual CTR over time. And when a model launch shows a gorgeous AUC gain, ask for the calibration slices before you pop the champagne.

A calibration dashboard shows that ads launched in the last 48 hours are over-predicted by 40% on average, while established ads are well calibrated. What does this do to the marketplace, who is affected, and what would you ask the team to consider?

  • Effect: new ads win auctions they shouldn't (inflated scores) and pay too little per click (inflated denominator). Established advertisers lose delivery and pay more. Pebble loses revenue, and users see less relevant ads. Pretty much everyone loses except the new ads.
  • Why it happens: new ads have no history, so the model falls back on priors (category, advertiser) that might be too optimistic. Their ID features are also untrained (Chapter 6 covers cold start).
  • Options: a calibration layer segmented by ad age; smoothing new-ad predictions toward a conservative prior; a deliberate, bounded exploration budget for new ads, since some over-exposure is how the system learns about them (Chapter 5); and monitoring whether the over-prediction fades as data comes in.
  • The question to ask: is the over-exposure intentional (exploration) or accidental? If it's intentional, who's paying for it? Some platforms explicitly discount new-ad pricing, or fund exploration from a dedicated budget.

What you built

A calibration layer, fitted to your own traffic, that your stack can apply after ranking. And now you know why that innocent little division by pCTR makes calibration a money problem, not just a modeling one.

So far your stack has been flying blind: a hand-written heuristic, no memory of what happened yesterday, no way to learn. That changes next. To learn anything, first we have to write things down, and it turns out writing things down correctly is way harder than it sounds. See ya in Chapter 4.
