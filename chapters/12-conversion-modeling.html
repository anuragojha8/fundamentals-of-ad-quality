Chapter 12 · Part IV · The Models

Conversion Modeling

In which advertisers inform me they wanted customers, not clicks. (Rude, but fair.)

Every ad your stack has served so far was sold per click. And for eleven chapters, we've acted like clicks were the whole point. They're not. A click is just a means to an end. Sprout wants meal-kit subscriptions. Nimbus wants bank accounts. Orbit wants installs. Nobody's out there celebrating a really great click.

So modern ad platforms let advertisers optimize for the outcome they actually want, and the platform takes on the job of predicting it. In this chapter we build the post-click conversion model (pCVR) your stack needs. These labels are rare, late, and, in Pebble's simulator, recorded only after a click. View-through conversions exist elsewhere; this chapter doesn't model them. Three problems, one chapter. Let's go.

12.1Bidding for outcomes

With conversion optimization, an advertiser can state a target cost per acquisition (CPA): "Aim for $40 in spend per customer." Pebble's simplified controller turns that goal into an effective maximum CPC, target CPA × predicted post-click CVR. The auction then scores the ad using both predictions:

\[ \text{eCPM} = \text{CPA}_{\text{target}} \times \text{pCTR} \times \text{pCVR} \times 1000 \]

Here pCVR = P(conversion | click). The score is a bid-weighted value, not Pebble's expected revenue. Pebble still charges the clearing CPC in this toy auction. Predictions affect which ads win and the prices they may face, while realized CPA also depends on competition, budgets, actual conversions, and the controller. A target is not a guarantee.

Sprout's target CPA is $40. For some impression, pCTR = 2% and pCVR = 5%. What's the eCPM?

Hint

40 × 0.02 × 0.05 × 1000.

40 × 0.02 × 0.05 × 1000 = $40 eCPM, or 4 cents of auction score per impression. The effective maximum CPC is $40 × 0.05 = $2; the clearing CPC can be lower. The score multiplies two predictions, so if both are 20% too high, it is 44% too high. Fun!

12.2Sparse labels

Let's look at what we're working with. Your ten days of logs have about 180,000 impressions, roughly 7,000 clicks, and only a few hundred conversions. That's one conversion for every ~350 impressions. A model with a weight per ad ID gets, on average, one or two conversions per ad to learn from. One or two! You can't learn much from that.

That's why conversion models lean hard on shared signal: category, advertiser, and user-level features, plus tricks that borrow strength from the much more plentiful click data (we'll get to one in Section 12.4).

12.3Delayed labels

A click on a Gaming ad turns into an install within minutes. A click on Nimbus's bank ad turns into a new account within days, if it ever does. In Pebble's world, the average delay ranges from 2 hours (Gaming) all the way to 5 days (Finance). So when your training job runs, the most recent clicks simply haven't had time to convert yet. You saw this back in Chapter 4: they get labeled 0 when the honest answer is "not yet."

But if you know roughly how the delays are distributed, you can correct for it. If delays are exponential with mean \(\mu\), then the fraction of eventual conversions you've already seen, \(t\) hours after the click, is

\[ F(t) = 1 - e^{-t/\mu} \]

So instead of dividing observed conversions by clicks, you divide by the expected number of clicks that have had time to convert: \( \widehat{\text{CVR}} = \frac{\text{conversions observed}}{\sum_{\text{clicks}} F(t_{\text{now}} - t_{\text{click}})} \). In other words, you give the slow ones partial credit for still being on their way.

Delayed feedback is behind a lot of "your model under-predicts for advertiser X" escalations. Advertisers with long conversion cycles (finance, travel, B2B, big purchases) get systematically under-predicted by models trained on fresh data, so they under-deliver, and they're usually the ones spending the most. Ask how the conversion model handles delay. The usual answers: wait N days before using data (stale but clean), reweight or correct for the expected delay, or model the delay distribution explicitly (Chapelle, 2014, "Modeling Delayed Feedback in Display Advertising"). And ask what happens to conversions that show up after the attribution window closes.

12.4Sample selection bias and ESMM

Okay, here's the sneakiest problem of the three. pCVR is defined as P(conversion | click). So the obvious way to train it is on clicked impressions only, right? That's where the conversions are!

But the auction needs pCVR for every candidate before a click happens. Clicked impressions are a selected subset of logged impressions, and the logs themselves omit ads never shown. In Pebble's simulation, click-prone users and creatives have different post-click conversion rates, so a clicked-only model may extrapolate badly where clicks are rare. A correctly specified conditional model with enough support could still work; selection alone does not prove bias. The risk is learning in one region and serving in another.

ESMM (Ma et al., 2018) addresses this with a multi-task model. It trains pCTR on clicks and the product pCTR × pCVR = P(click and post-click conversion | x), also called pCTCVR, on conversion labels across logged impressions. That shared evidence can help pCVR outside the clicked subset. It cannot identify outcomes for ads never shown, recover unobserved conversions, or guarantee calibration where click support is tiny; later work studies remaining estimation bias. View-through conversions are a different label.

A naive pCVR model (trained on clicks only) is well calibrated on clicked impressions. Why can it still be biased in the auction?

  • The attribution window is too short
  • The auction applies it to all candidates, and non-clicking users and less clickbaity ads convert differently from the clicked population it learned from
  • It has too few conversions
  • pCTR is miscalibrated

Calibration on clicked logs does not establish calibration for every candidate slice. In this simulation ESMM uses joint click-and-conversion labels across logged impressions to reduce that gap. Inverse propensity weighting can help only with valid click propensities and overlap; it cannot conjure labels for unshown ads or missing consent.

12.5Switch on conversion bidding

Time to change how Pebble optimizes ads. In the treatment arm, each advertiser supplies a target CPA and your stack computes target CPA × pCTR × pCVR as its score. Check CPA adherence: actual spend divided by actual conversions compared with the target. A high CPA calls for investigation; a low one does not by itself prove under-delivery. Read it alongside conversion volume, spend, budget use, and advertiser value.

After launching conversion optimization, revenue is up 20%, but advertisers' actual CPA is 35% above target. Which model issue would you investigate first, and what else must you check?

  • Check pCVR overprediction, then compare clearing prices, budget use, conversion measurement and controller behavior
  • pCVR must be under-predicted
  • pCTR must be too low
  • No issue: revenue is up

Overpredicted pCVR can inflate effective bids and raise spend per conversion, so start with calibration on mature labels. But a target miss is not proof: competition, budgets, conversion measurement and the controller can produce the same symptom. Advertisers pay clearing CPC here, not the predicted conversion price.

Nimbus (a bank, where conversions take ~5 days) reports that under oCPM they get 40% fewer conversions than their budget should buy, while their actual CPA is well below target. What's happening, and what do you propose?

  • Hypothesis: delayed labels may make Nimbus's pCVR look too low, lowering effective bids and losing auctions. Low CPA plus under-delivery does not prove it; budget pacing, competition, eligibility and measurement could also explain the pattern.
  • Evidence to pull: pCVR calibration by label age (recent vs. matured), conversion delay, win rate, clearing CPC, budget use and measured conversion volume.
  • Fixes: delay correction or delayed-feedback modeling for slow advertisers; excluding immature negatives from training; a calibration layer segmented by vertical; and, in the short term, letting the autobidder's CPA controller raise Nimbus's bids.
  • Why it matters: systematically under-delivering for long-cycle verticals is both a revenue problem and a fairness problem. Finance and travel advertisers are often among the most valuable ones on the platform.

What you built

A conversion model (naive and ESMM), a delay correction using your observedFraction(), and conversion optimization in your auction, judged by spend, conversions, CPA and advertiser value against the stated goal.

There's a catch, though, and it's a big one. This whole chapter assumed every conversion comes back to you as a neat little record with a user attached to it. Out in the world, users say no to tracking, browsers drop cookies, and conversions arrive as coarse, noisy, delayed aggregates instead. Next time, we find out what your beautiful pCVR model is worth when the data stops being so generous.
