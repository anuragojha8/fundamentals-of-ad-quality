Chapter 11 · Part IV · The Models

Towers, Crosses, and Transformers

In which I build a much bigger model, and it humbles me.

Let's be honest about your logistic regression. It's a list of weights, one per feature string, all added up. Charming, reliable, and a bit of a golden retriever: it can only learn the interactions you spelled out for it by hand, like hour×cat.

Modern ranking models learn their interactions instead. They turn every ID into a vector, let those vectors mingle through special layers, and read the user's history with attention, the same mechanism at the heart of transformers (yes, the ChatGPT kind). This chapter builds one, piece by piece. And then it asks the only question that matters: does it actually beat your LR where it counts? The answer is going to surprise you a little. It surprised me.

11.1The anatomy

Embeddings

An embedding swaps an ID for a learned vector: 8 numbers per user, per ad, per category. Your LR learns one number per ad ("how clickable is ad 182, in general?"). An embedding learns how ad 182 is clickable: for which kinds of users, in which contexts. And vectors for similar things drift close together, so whatever the model learns about one ad partly rubs off on its lookalikes.

Cross layers

You could feed embeddings straight into an MLP (a stack of dense layers), and it'd work. But MLPs are weirdly bad at learning multiplicative interactions like "this age group × this category." A cross layer (from DCN, the Deep & Cross Network, Wang et al., 2017 and 2021) builds those interactions on purpose:

\[ x_{l+1} = x_0 \odot (W x_l + b) + x_l \]

Each layer multiplies the original input \(x_0\), element by element, with a learned linear transformation of the current layer. So every layer adds one more degree of feature interaction. It's the learned, automatic version of the crosses you built by hand in Chapter 6.

Target attention over history

Your LR summarizes a user's history with counts, like "3 of the last 12 posts were Food." But which history items actually matter depends on the ad you're considering. For a Food ad, the Food posts matter. For a Gaming ad, the gaming posts. Target attention (DIN, the Deep Interest Network, Alibaba, 2018) uses the candidate ad as a query, scores every history item against it, and takes a weighted average:

\[ \text{score}_i = \frac{h_i \cdot q}{\sqrt{d}}, \qquad \alpha = \text{softmax}(\text{score}), \qquad \text{pooled} = \sum_i \alpha_i h_i \]

That's the exact same attention that powers transformers. Sequence models like BST (Alibaba, 2019) and HSTU (Meta's "generative recommenders," 2024) stack many layers of it over long user histories. Those histories can run to thousands of events, and serving them fast enough is one of the hardest infrastructure problems in ads right now.

11.2Two towers vs. cross towers

Chapter 10's two-tower model kept the user and the ad strictly apart until one final dot product. That separation is exactly what made it fast enough for retrieval over millions of ads. The model above is the complete opposite. It's sometimes called a cross tower or early-interaction model: user and ad features mix from the very first layer, and attention even uses the ad to read the user's history. Way more expressive. And impossible to precompute, because every (user, ad) pair needs its own forward pass.

Two-towerCross tower
User–ad interactionOne dot product, at the endEverywhere, from the first layer
Ad side precomputable?Yes, into an ANN indexNo; one forward pass per pair
Candidates per requestMillionsTens to hundreds
Where it livesRetrieval, early rankingLate ranking

That's why your stack serves the deep model in two stages. A cheap early ranker (your LR) picks the top 30 by eCPM, and only those 30 get the expensive treatment. Yep, it's Chapter 1's funnel. Again. I told you it would keep coming back.

11.3Wide & Deep: don't throw away what you know

Deep models learn interactions from data. Your hand-built features encode interactions you already know about. Why choose? Wide & Deep (Google, 2016) keeps both: a linear "wide" part over your engineered features, added to the deep network's output, all trained together. The deep part generalizes. The wide part memorizes specific, reliable patterns, and it's often the thing that keeps a deep model honest on data it hasn't seen much of.

11.4Train your deep ranker

Okay, let's play. Switch the pieces on and off, train, and keep an eye on two numbers. Offline AUC is the headline number a model review would show you. Within-request accuracy against the truth is measured on the 30 candidates the model actually ranks in production. That second one is the number the auction actually feels. Your LR is the baseline to beat.

Common trap

"The deep model has higher AUC, ship it!" Hold on. AUC compares every clicked impression with every non-clicked one, across all users. A model can raise it just by getting better at figuring out which users click a lot (ID embeddings are great at that), while getting no better, or even worse, at picking which ad to show this particular user. And the auction only ever makes that second decision. So always ask for within-request metrics (GAUC) right next to global AUC, and never, ever skip the A/B test.

11.6Does it win? Data decides

Here's the experiment that actually matters: an A/B test of your deep ranker against your LR, both trained on the same logs. Then do it again with three times as much training data.

Why would that make a difference? Because deep models have way more parameters than an LR. With a little data, they tend to memorize. With a lot of data, they generalize. Where your stack sits on that curve decides whether all this fancy architecture actually pays off. (On the reference stack, the deep model loses about 7% of revenue with 10 days of data and catches up to parity at 30. Your stack might tell a different story.)

In your job

At big companies, architecture launches are usually worth somewhere between +0.1% and +0.5% in NE, and they cost real money in serving compute. So when someone pitches a bigger model, ask: how much data does it need to beat the current one, and do we actually have it? What does it cost per 1,000 requests to serve? Which stage of the funnel runs it, and on how many candidates? And does it win within requests, or just in global AUC? The best teams scale data, features, and model size together. Not the model alone.

Why can't a cross-tower model with target attention be used for retrieval over millions of ads?

  • It's less accurate than a two-tower
  • The user representation depends on the candidate ad (attention uses the ad as the query), so nothing can be precomputed; every pair needs a full forward pass
  • It doesn't use embeddings
  • It needs conversion labels

With target attention, "the user" is a different vector for every candidate ad. There's no single user vector to look up in an index, so the cost scales with (users × ads). Totally affordable for 30 candidates. Completely impossible for 3 million.

A deep model beats the production LR offline (AUC +0.02) but loses 5% revenue in an A/B test. Which follow-up is most likely to explain it?

  • Train it for more epochs
  • Compare within-request ranking accuracy and calibration on the candidates it ranks in production, not just global AUC on logged impressions
  • Rerun the A/B test until it's positive
  • Reduce its latency

Offline AUC on logged impressions measures something different from what the auction actually does: it's cross-user pairs, and only on ads that were already shown. Evaluate the decision the model really makes (within-request, over the real candidate set), plus its calibration there. Which is exactly what you just did above.

Your ranking team wants to replace the current model with a transformer over each user's last 2,000 events. It's +0.4% NE offline and costs 6× the serving compute. Draft the questions and the plan you'd bring to the review.

  • Is the gain real where it matters? GAUC and calibration on the production candidate set, slices for heavy vs. light users (short histories get very little out of long-sequence models), and an A/B test with revenue, advertiser value, and user-experience guardrails.
  • Cost vs. value. 6× compute on how many candidates? Can it run on fewer (a stricter early stage)? Can the sequence encoding be cached per user and shared across candidates (user-side precomputation)? What's the dollar cost per 1% NE, and how does that stack up against other roadmap items?
  • Latency and freshness. Where do those 2,000 events come from at serve time, and how fresh are they? A stale history throws away a lot of the point.
  • Plan: a small-traffic A/B first; distill it into a cheaper model if the win is real (Chapter 13); then ramp with latency and timeout monitoring.

What you built

A deep ranker with embeddings, a DCN cross layer, target attention using your very own attentionWeights(), and a wide part carrying your hand-built features, served in two stages behind your LR. More importantly, you know how to tell whether it's worth shipping, which is more than a lot of launch decks can say.

Now, every model we've built so far predicts clicks. But here's a secret most advertisers would love you to know: they don't actually want clicks. They want customers. Next time, we chase conversions, the rare, slow, frustrating labels that advertisers actually pay for.
