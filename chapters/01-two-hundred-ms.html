Chapter 1 · Part I · The Marketplace

Two Hundred Milliseconds

In which I accidentally run three thousand auctions before breakfast.

Okay, so here's a fun fact that ruins scrolling forever: every time someone opens Pebble and starts thumbing through photos, a whole marketplace spins up, holds an auction, picks a winner, sets a price, writes everything down for posterity, and packs up. In about 200 milliseconds. You blink slower than that.

Three feed slots ahead of our scroller are reserved for ads. Those slots need to be filled with ads that are allowed to be there, that this person might actually care about, and that are priced correctly. And the whole decision has to be written down so the system can get smarter tomorrow. No pressure.

In this chapter we'll follow one of those requests from start to finish. We'll also start the project that runs through the entire book: your very own ad stack. It's a small but real ad system that runs right here in your browser, against a simulated Pebble with 4,000 users, 60 advertisers, and about 340 ads. By the end of the chapter, it'll be serving (simulated) traffic. Pretty cool, right?

1.1The journey of one ad request

Here's what happens between the scroll and the ad, in order. Every production ad system I've seen has some version of these stages. The names change from company to company, because naming things is hard.

  1. Request. The app asks the ad server for ads, and sends along who the user is and the context: device, time, which surface, how many slots.
  2. Eligibility. Most ads can't be shown to this person right now. The campaign might not be running today, or it's targeting another country or age group, or its budget is spent, or the user has already seen it five times this morning (that's a frequency cap, and it's why you don't see the same car ad twelve times in a row). Filtering is cheap, exact, and happens first.
  3. Retrieval (a.k.a. candidate generation). A real Pebble would have millions of ads. Retrieval uses cheap, fast models to cut that down to a few hundred or a few thousand plausible candidates. Chapter 10.
  4. Ranking. Models predict, for each candidate, the probability of whatever the advertiser cares about: a click (pCTR), a conversion (pCVR), or something bad, like the user hiding the ad. Big systems do this twice: a light model over many candidates, then a heavy one over the survivors.
  5. Auction. Predictions get combined with bids into a score, winners get picked, and each winner gets a price. Chapter 2.
  6. Serve. The winners go back to the app and show up in the feed.
  7. Log. Everything gets written down: which ads were candidates, what we predicted, what we showed, and later, what the user actually did. This is the raw material for every model in this book. Chapter 4.

1.2Why a funnel? The latency budget

Let's start with the dumbest possible design. You have one great model. Why not just score every ad with it and pick the best?

Because the feed won't wait for you. The ad server gets something like 100 milliseconds end to end, and ranking gets a slice of that. A heavy ranking model might need half a millisecond per candidate. That sounds like nothing, until you do the math: two thousand candidates at 0.5 ms each is a full second. Your user has scrolled past, closed the app, and started a new hobby.

So we do the next best thing: a funnel. Cheap models go over lots of candidates, expensive models go over a few. Problem solved?

Not quite. Here's the catch, and it's the single most important idea in this chapter: every stage can only lose good ads, never find them. If retrieval throws away the best ad, no ranking model downstream, however fancy, can bring it back.

Play with the tradeoff below. The simulation draws 20,000 eligible ads, each with a hidden true value. Each stage estimates those values with some noise (cheaper models are noisier) and keeps its top K. Try to find the sweet spot without blowing the budget.

The funnel under a deadline
Keep more candidates at each stage and the best ad survives more often, but latency grows. Requests that blow the budget time out and show no ad at all.
Intuition

There's a sweet spot. Make the funnel too narrow and you throw away good ads early. Make it too wide and you blow the latency budget. Timeouts become blank slots, and blank slots make exactly zero dollars. Also, notice something sneaky: a better model in an early stage often beats a bigger model in the last one. The late model can only choose from what it's handed.

In your job

Here's a secret: almost every ads-ranking proposal is secretly a latency proposal. "Let's use a bigger model" really means "let's spend more milliseconds per candidate." So I always ask three things. What's the p99 latency impact? (The slowest 1% of requests are the ones that time out.) What's the compute cost? And which stage are we improving? A shiny win in late ranking can't fix a recall problem in retrieval.

Your retrieval stage keeps 500 of 2 million eligible ads. The late-stage ranking team ships a model that's way better at ordering its 50 inputs. Which problem can this model not fix?

  • Showing the second-best of the 50 finalists instead of the best
  • The best ad for this user was never among the 500 retrieved
  • Prices being set with inaccurate predictions
  • Showing irrelevant ads that made it through retrieval

A downstream stage only sees what the upstream stages pass along. Losing a good candidate early is a recall problem, and here's the annoying part: it's invisible to late-stage metrics. The late ranker's offline numbers look great on the candidates it actually sees. That's why retrieval teams measure recall against a stronger reference model (Chapter 10).

Your late-stage ranker costs 0.5 ms of compute per candidate, and the ranking budget is 40 ms. Ignoring parallelism and overheads, what's the most candidates it can score?

Hint

Budget ÷ cost per candidate. Yes, it's that simple.

40 ms ÷ 0.5 ms = 80 candidates. Real systems batch candidates on GPUs, so cost isn't perfectly linear, but the principle holds. The late ranker's candidate count is set by its cost per candidate. Double the model size and you roughly halve how many candidates it gets to look at.

1.3Build: the eligibility filter

Alright, enough talking. Time to build.

Your stack's very first job is deciding which ads are allowed to show up at all. In our simulated Pebble, each ad has flight dates (it runs from startDay up to, but not including, endDay) and optional targeting: a list of allowed countries and a list of allowed age buckets. null means "anyone's fine."

Countries are numbered 0 = US, 1 = UK, 2 = BR. Age buckets are 0 = 18–24, 1 = 25–34, 2 = 35–49, 3 = 50+. Write the filter below and hit Run tests. The tests check your function against the real ad server on 400 (ad, user, day) combinations, so no pressure. Well, a little pressure.

In your job

Eligibility looks boring. Honestly, it's where a lot of the scariest bugs live. An eligibility bug that shows alcohol ads to teenagers, or keeps a campaign running past its end date, isn't a "quality regression." It's a policy incident, or a very awkward conversation with an advertiser's finance team. Eligibility code is usually owned by a different team than ranking, and it deserves its own tests and its own monitoring.

1.4Build: your first pCTR, no model required

The auction needs, for every candidate, a guess at the click-through rate: the probability that this user clicks this ad if we put it in the top slot. Now, you don't have a model yet. You don't even have logs (those come in Chapter 4). What you do have is last month's dashboard, and some good old common sense.

Last month's dashboard
Top-slot CTR by ad category, from a month when ads were served at random. This is the prior.catCTR array your function receives.

You also have each user's engagement history: the categories of the 12 organic posts they most recently liked or commented on, most recent first. This is a really strong signal. Someone who's been double-tapping hiking photos all week is probably more into Trailhead's boots right now than into a mortgage.

So here's the challenge. If I asked you to predict clicks with just these two things, how would you do it? Write a heuristic that starts from the category's CTR and nudges it using the history. There's no single right answer here. The tests only require that you return valid probabilities and that you order ads better than a coin flip, measured against the true click probabilities (which only a simulator gets to know). See how high you can push that score. I'm competitive about this, and you should be too.

Going deeper: why not just use each ad's own CTR?

You totally could, and Chapter 6 turns it into a counting feature. But on its own it has two problems. New ads have no history (the cold-start problem, which is as fun as it sounds). And an ad's CTR averages over everyone it was shown to, so it can't tell you whether this person will click. Personalization needs signals about the user–ad pair, like the history match in your heuristic.

Common trap

"Our CTR is 3.4%, that's great!" Great compared to what? A CTR on its own tells you almost nothing. It depends on ad load, slot position, category mix, and which users got the traffic. What actually matters is the gap to a better system, and in real life you only ever see that gap through an experiment (Chapter 9). The oracle here is a luxury of simulation. It tells us how much headroom better predictions could unlock. And that headroom is basically the business case for the rest of this book.

In the day simulation, the oracle serves the same requests with perfect click predictions and gets more clicks and more revenue. Where does the extra revenue come from?

  • Advertisers bid more for better predictions
  • Better predictions put ads that users actually want to click in the slots, so more impressions turn into billable clicks
  • The oracle shows more ads
  • The oracle charges higher prices per click

With CPC bidding, Pebble gets paid per click. The auction ranks by bid × pCTR, so when pCTR is wrong, the auction hands slots to ads nobody clicks. That's lost revenue for Pebble, wasted attention for users, and missed customers for advertisers. Better prediction is that rare lever that helps all three at once. (Per-click prices can actually move either way. Chapter 3 explains why.)

1.6Who owns what

One last thing before we wrap up. At a real company, the stages you just wired together in a few minutes are owned by different teams, and each team has its own favorite metric. Knowing who owns what, and what can quietly break in each place, is half the job.

StageTypical ownerTheir headline metricWhat can go wrong silently
Eligibility & targetingAdvertiser products / deliveryDelivery correctnessAds shown outside targeting; campaigns not spending
RetrievalRetrieval / candidate generationRecall@K vs. a referenceGood ads never reach ranking
RankingAds quality / rankingAUC, normalized entropy, calibrationOffline gains that don't move online metrics
AuctionMarketplace / monetizationRevenue, advertiser ROI, price stabilityMiscalibrated predictions becoming mispricing
Logging & dataData infraFreshness, completenessLabel bugs that corrupt every model at once

The retrieval team wants to double the number of candidates passed to ranking. Their offline analysis says recall of the eventual winners goes from 91% to 96%. Infra estimates p99 ad-server latency goes from 140 ms to 175 ms. The budget is 200 ms. What do you want to know before you say yes?

Here's what I'd want covered:

  • Tails, not averages. What's the p99.9, and what's the timeout rate today versus projected? Timeouts are blank slots, and blank slots are zero revenue. Are the slow requests piling up in certain markets or on older phones?
  • Where does the recall gain turn into money? Recall of winners is nice, but what's the expected revenue or advertiser-value gain? Five points of recall on low-value requests is worth less than two points on high-value ones.
  • Cost. Twice the candidates means roughly twice the ranking compute. What does that cost per day? And is there a cheaper way to get the same recall, like a better retrieval model instead of a wider funnel?
  • Headroom for everyone else. If retrieval eats 35 ms of the budget, what's left for the next ranking-model launch? Latency is a shared budget, and somebody needs to own it.
  • How will we know? An A/B test measuring revenue, advertiser value, and timeout rate, with latency guardrails.
