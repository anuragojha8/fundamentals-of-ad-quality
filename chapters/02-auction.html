The Auction

In which forty thousand advertisers fight over three seats, and I try to make everyone pay a fair price.

So here's a question that sounds dumb but isn't: why do ads need an auction at all? Why doesn't Pebble just put up a price list, say $1 a click, and let advertisers form an orderly queue?

Because Pebble has no idea what a click is worth. To Nimbus, the neobank, a click that turns into a new account might be worth $10. To Orbit, a mobile game, a click might be worth 30 cents. Each advertiser knows its own number, and each has every reason in the world to keep it a secret. (Would you tell the store the maximum you'd pay? Exactly.) An auction is a clever little mechanism for getting them to reveal that number anyway, through their bids.

2.1First price or second price?

Let's start as simple as possible: one slot, and every ad is equally likely to be clicked. Everyone submits a sealed bid, and the highest bid wins. Easy. The only real question is what the winner pays.

  • In a first-price auction, the winner pays its own bid.
  • In a second-price auction, the winner pays the second-highest bid, i.e. the least it could have bid and still won.

First price sounds like it makes more money, right? The winner pays more! But advertisers aren't naive. If you pay what you bid, you'll bid less than the click is worth to you (this is called bid shading), and now everyone's playing a guessing game about how much to shade. Don't take my word for it. You're Trailhead, and you know what a click is worth to you. The other bidders are fixed. Find the bid that maximizes your expected surplus: value minus price, averaged over lots of auctions.

Intuition

Here's the magic of second price: bidding your true value is the best strategy, no matter what anyone else does. Your bid only decides whether you win, never what you pay. Bid above your value and you risk winning at a price that's more than the click is worth to you. Bid below it and you lose auctions you'd have happily won. Economists call this truthfulness (or incentive compatibility, if you want to sound fancy at dinner parties). It makes life simpler for advertisers, and it lets the platform learn what clicks are actually worth.

In a single-slot second-price auction, Nimbus values a click at $4.00. The other bids are $2.50, $3.10, and $1.80. What should Nimbus bid, and what does it pay if it wins?

  • Bid $3.11, pay $3.11
  • Bid $4.00, pay $3.10
  • Bid $4.00, pay $4.00
  • Bid $4.00, pay $1.80

Bid the true value, $4.00. Nimbus wins and pays the second-highest bid, $3.10, pocketing $0.90 of surplus. Any bid above $3.10 gives the exact same outcome, which is precisely why shading is pointless here.

2.2Ranking by expected value: eCPM

Now let's drop the unrealistic part. Ads are not equally likely to be clicked. And here's the wrinkle: Pebble charges per click (that's CPC bidding), but what it actually sells is impressions, a slot in someone's feed. An ad bidding $5 per click that nobody ever clicks earns Pebble a grand total of zero dollars. So the auction ranks by expected revenue per impression:

\[ \text{score} = \text{bid} \times \text{pCTR}, \qquad \text{eCPM} = \text{bid} \times \text{pCTR} \times 1000 \]

eCPM ("effective cost per mille," because the ad industry loves Latin) is the same score expressed per thousand impressions. It's the common currency that lets you compare an ad bidding per click with one bidding per impression or per conversion.

And this, right here, is the moment predictions enter the auction. From now on, Pebble's revenue and every advertiser's position depend on pCTR just as much as on bids. Remember that. It's going to haunt us.

Sprout bids $1.20 per click and its predicted CTR is 2.5%. What's its eCPM?

Hint

bid × pCTR × 1000, with pCTR as a probability (0.025).

1.20 × 0.025 × 1000 = $30. Pebble expects to earn $30 for every thousand times it shows this ad.

2.3Many slots: the generalized second-price auction

A Pebble feed load has three ad slots, and they're not created equal. People see the first one a lot more than the third (Chapter 5 measures exactly how much). The classic design for multiple slots, used for decades in search and feed ads, is the generalized second-price auction (GSP):

  1. Rank candidates by score = bid × pCTR.
  2. The top-ranked ad gets slot 1, the next gets slot 2, and so on.
  3. Each winner pays, per click, the least it could have bid and still kept its spot. That works out to the score of the ad just below it, divided by its own pCTR:
\[ \text{price}_i = \frac{\text{bid}_{i+1} \cdot \text{pCTR}_{i+1}}{\text{pCTR}_i} \]

Read that formula again, slowly. Your price depends on your own pCTR. If the model thinks your ad is more clickable, you pay less per click for the same slot. That's on purpose: it rewards relevant ads. But it also means that when the model is wrong about your ad, your price is wrong too. Which is, conveniently, the entire plot of Chapter 3.

Hint

Scores: 2.00×0.03, 3.00×0.015, 1.00×0.04. The winner pays the runner-up's score divided by its own pCTR.

Scores: Lumen 0.060, Orbit 0.045, Trailhead 0.040. Lumen wins and pays 0.045 / 0.03 = $1.50 per click. And poor Orbit bid the most and still lost, because its ad is predicted to be half as clickable as Lumen's. Money can't buy you love, or feed slots, apparently.

Going deeper: GSP isn't truthful, and VCG is

Plot twist: with multiple slots, GSP loses the second-price magic. An advertiser can sometimes do better by bidding below value to drop into a cheaper slot. The VCG mechanism (Vickrey–Clarke–Groves) fixes this by charging each winner the harm its presence does to everyone else, which makes truthful bidding optimal again. Some large platforms have used VCG-style pricing for exactly this reason. In practice it matters less than you'd think, because most advertisers now use autobidding: the platform bids on their behalf toward a goal like "get me conversions at $40 each." A lot of modern auction research is about designing well in that world. The classic analyses are Edelman, Ostrovsky and Schwarz (2007) and Varian (2007).

Alright, you've been using my auction long enough. Let's replace it with yours.

Your function gets the candidates, each with a bid and a pctr, plus the number of slots and a reserve price, a minimum eCPM an ad has to clear to be shown at all. (More on that in a second.) The tests include the edge cases that bite real auction code: ties, a single bidder, a reserve nobody clears, and never, ever charging more than the bid. Advertisers get very upset about that last one.

Common trap

Ranking by bid alone. It sounds fair ("highest bidder wins, duh"), and it's usually what people build first. But it hands slots to ads nobody clicks. Pebble earns less, users waste their attention, and low-quality advertisers get to buy their way to the top. Try it in the widget above: switch the ranking to "by bid only" and watch expected revenue drop.

2.5Reserve prices: the platform's lever

When only a few advertisers show up for a request, second prices can get embarrassingly low. A lone bidder pays nothing at all! A reserve price sets a floor: an ad needs an eCPM of at least the reserve to be shown, and the last winner pays at least the reserve. Crank it up and prices rise, but some slots go empty. Empty slots are lost revenue, though they also mean fewer weak ads in front of users. Tradeoffs everywhere.

Let's test it on your own stack. Run your auction at different reserve prices over a day of Pebble traffic. Then run the sweep again in a thin market, where only two ads compete for three slots, which is what a niche audience or a brand-new surface looks like. Then pick a reserve for production.

In your job

Reserve prices are one of the very few levers that change prices directly, so get ready for some spirited meetings. Sales worries about advertisers who stop spending, finance loves the revenue, and quality teams love having fewer weak ads. Good reserve policies have two things in common. They're usually personalized (different floors for different users, surfaces, or countries, learned from data). And they're judged on long-run advertiser retention, not next week's revenue. An advertiser you price out today might just not come back.

You raise Pebble's reserve and revenue goes up 4% in a week-long A/B test. Which effect would the test most likely miss?

  • Fewer slots being filled
  • Higher prices per click
  • Small advertisers slowly reducing budgets or churning because their costs rose
  • Users seeing fewer ads

Advertisers are slow. Budgets get set monthly, and campaigns get reviewed by humans (or autobidders) over weeks. A short test sees prices go up but not the quiet exodus that follows. That's why marketplace teams use longer holdouts and advertiser-level metrics like spend retention and ROI. Chapter 15 comes back to this short-term vs. long-term tension, because it's everywhere.

2.6What advertisers actually bid for

So far everyone bids per click. Real platforms offer a whole menu, and each option is really a promise about what the advertiser pays for:

Notice the pattern? As you go down the table, the advertiser takes on less risk and Pebble takes on more. Every step down needs another accurate prediction. Your stack runs on CPC for now. In Chapter 12, you'll build a conversion model and flip on oCPM.

Two weeks after your team launched a new pCTR model, Lumen's account manager escalates: Lumen's cost per click rose 20%, even though their bids didn't change. Nothing else changed in the marketplace. Using the GSP price formula, give two different explanations, and say what data you'd pull to tell them apart.

From price = (runner-up's bid × runner-up's pCTR) / Lumen's pCTR, the price goes up if the top of that fraction goes up or the bottom goes down.

  • The model now predicts a lower pCTR for Lumen. Maybe the new model thinks Lumen's creatives are less clickable. If it's right, Lumen was being undercharged before. If it's wrong (say, a feature bug for Lumen's category), Lumen is now overcharged. Pull Lumen's average pCTR before and after, next to their actual CTR. In other words, the calibration of predictions against outcomes for Lumen's ads.
  • The model now predicts a higher pCTR for Lumen's competitors. The runner-up's score went up, so Lumen pays more to hold the same spot. Pull the scores of the ads ranked just below Lumen's.
  • A composition shift. Lumen might just be winning different impressions now (different users or slots) where prices are naturally higher. Compare like with like: same slot, similar users.

The underlying point, which Chapter 3 makes precise: a pCTR model change is a pricing change. That's why launches get reviewed for per-advertiser calibration, not just overall AUC.
