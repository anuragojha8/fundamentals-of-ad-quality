Chapter 8 · Part III · Learning

Losses and Model Heads

In which I tell the model what "good" means, and it takes me extremely literally.

In Chapter 7 you used log loss without asking a single question about it. I let you. It was a big day, you were training your first model, I didn't want to ruin it.

But here's the thing: the loss function is the only thing a model truly listens to. It's the model's entire definition of "good." And different losses produce models that are good at genuinely different things. Some rank beautifully but can't price. Some price well but rank a little worse. Some are built for counts, or for picking one item out of millions. This chapter is about telling them apart, and about how modern models bolt several losses onto one network using heads.

8.2What each loss pays attention to

When you're sizing up a loss, two properties matter most. First, how hard does it punish confident mistakes? Second, does it care about the actual value of the prediction, or only the order? Play with both below.

Loss and gradient as the prediction changes
Left: the loss for one example as the predicted probability p moves. Right: how hard the loss pushes on the logit (|∂loss/∂z|), which is what actually drives learning.
Intuition

Log loss keeps pushing hard when the model is confidently wrong: predict 1% for something that actually happened, and the gradient is close to 1. MSE on a probability does the opposite. It stops pushing right there, because its gradient has a factor of p(1−p) that shrinks to zero. That's why MSE learns rare events so sluggishly. It basically shrugs at the click it predicted at 1%.

The model predicted a 1% chance of a click, and the user clicked. What's the log loss for this example? (Natural log.)

Hint

With y = 1, the loss is −log p.

−ln(0.01) ≈ 4.605. Now compare a correct, confident prediction: predicting 1% for a non-click costs −ln(0.99) ≈ 0.01. So one surprise click costs as much as about 460 correctly predicted non-clicks. That asymmetry is exactly what keeps log loss honest about rare events.

8.3Order vs. value: the pairwise loss

A pairwise loss takes two ads shown in the same request, one clicked and one not, and asks just one thing: did the clicked one score higher? It only depends on the difference between the scores, so if you add the same constant to every score, nothing changes. That's exactly what ranking needs.

And it's also exactly why a pairwise model is useless for pricing. It has zero reason to put its scores on a probability scale. It'll happily tell you ad A beats ad B, and have no idea whether either one gets clicked 2% or 20% of the time.

Shift both scores
Two ads in one request. The first was clicked, the second wasn't. Move both logits together, or change the gap between them.

A model trained only with a pairwise loss ranks ads within each request very well. Why can't your auction use its outputs directly as pCTR?

  • It can't rank ads
  • Its scores are only meaningful relative to each other, not as probabilities, so bid × score isn't expected revenue
  • It's too slow to serve
  • It needs conversion data

Your auction multiplies pCTR by a bid, and your prices divide by pCTR. Both need calibrated values. A pairwise model's scores could all shift by +3 and its loss wouldn't even notice. Production systems that want pairwise ranking quality usually combine it with a pointwise log loss (a weighted sum of the two), or calibrate afterwards.

8.4Poisson: when the label is a count

Sometimes the label isn't yes-or-no, it's a count: purchases per click (some people buy twice), app opens in the week after an install, clicks per thousand impressions in an aggregated row. Poisson loss treats the model's output as a rate, \( \lambda = e^z \), and asks it to match the average count.

Two nice things about it. Its gradient, \( \lambda - y \), looks just like log loss's \( p - y \). And for rare events (rates well below 1), it behaves almost exactly like log loss. You'll see that for yourself in a second.

8.5Build: losses and their gradients

Your stack's trainer handles the pairwise loss itself, since it needs to build pairs from the same request by grouping rows. For the pointwise losses, it asks your function for the loss and its gradient with respect to the logit. The tests check your gradients numerically: they nudge z by a tiny amount and watch how the loss moves. So no faking it.

Now for the fun experiment. Train the exact same logistic regression on the exact same logs four times, once per loss, and compare. Two kinds of AUC show up here. The usual AUC compares every impression with every other impression. Within-request AUC only compares ads shown in the same request, which is the decision the auction actually makes. 8.6Model heads and multi-task learning

A real Pebble ranking model has to predict a bunch of things at once. Will the user click? Convert? Hide the ad? Watch the video? You could train a separate model for each, but that's expensive and kind of wasteful, since they're all learning about the same users and ads. So modern systems share most of the network and attach a small head for each task: a final layer (or a few) that turns the shared representation into one specific prediction, with its own loss.

Sharing is great for sparse tasks. Conversions are rare, but the click task feeds the shared layers tons of signal about which users like which ads, and the conversion head gets to ride along for free. But sharing can also backfire. Tasks that disagree can pull the shared layers in different directions, which is called negative transfer (basically, roommates who can't agree on the thermostat). Architectures like MMoE (Multi-gate Mixture-of-Experts, Google, 2018) and PLE (Tencent, 2020) give each head its own weighted mix of shared "expert" sub-networks to keep the peace.

There's also the calibration head: a small final layer (sometimes one per segment) trained to fix the scale of the main output. It's the learned cousin of the calibration layer you built back in Chapter 3.

Your team adds a hide head to the click model, with total loss = L_click + β·L_hide. They raise β from 0.1 to 1.0 and offline click AUC drops slightly. What's the right way to decide β?

  • Pick the β with the best click AUC
  • Pick the β with the best hide AUC
  • Evaluate the ranking score that combines the heads, online, on the metrics you care about (revenue, hides, retention)
  • β doesn't matter; heads are independent

Task weights trade one objective against another inside the shared layers. A small drop in click AUC might be totally worth a big drop in hides, or it might not. That's a product tradeoff, and it has to be judged on the combined outcome, usually in an A/B test with guardrails. Chapter 15 is all about owning that objective.

A research team proposes replacing Pebble's pointwise log-loss pCTR model with a listwise ranking loss. Offline, within-request ranking (GAUC) improves by 1%. What are your concerns, and what would you ask for?

  • Pricing. The auction needs calibrated pCTR, both for ranking by bid × pCTR and for setting prices. A listwise loss doesn't produce probabilities. Ask how calibration will be restored (a combined loss, a calibration head, post-hoc calibration) and ask to see calibration slices.
  • Cross-request decisions. Reserve prices, ad load (ad vs. organic), and CPM-vs-CPC competition all compare scores across requests and bid types. Only calibrated values make those comparisons meaningful.
  • The metric matches the loss. Of course a loss that optimizes GAUC improves GAUC. Ask for revenue-weighted metrics and an online test.
  • A combined objective (pointwise + pairwise) often gets most of the ranking gain while keeping calibration intact. Ask whether they tried it.

What you built

Your loss functions now power your stack's trainer, and you've seen what each one actually optimizes: log loss for calibrated probabilities, pairwise for order, Poisson for counts. Your production model stays on log loss, because your auction prices with it, and a model that can't price is a very expensive way to sort things.

Throughout this chapter I kept saying things like "AUC went up" and "calibration is off" as if those were obviously the right ways to keep score. They're not obvious, and they can absolutely lie to you. Next time, we figure out how to tell whether a model is actually better. It's the chapter I'd make every PM read twice.
