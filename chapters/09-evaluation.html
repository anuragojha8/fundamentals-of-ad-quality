Chapter 9 · Part III · Learning

Evaluation, Offline and Online

In which my model aces the test and then flunks the job interview.

So you've got a shiny trained logistic regression sitting in your stack, while a humble hand-written heuristic still serves production. Should you swap them? It's tempting: the new one has machine learning in it and everything.

Maybe! But a nicer-sounding model hasn't earned a launch yet. In this chapter you'll build the two metrics every ads ranking review opens with, read them against the simulator's truth, slice them up, and then run a randomized A/B experiment on your own traffic. At the end, you decide whether to ship it, and that choice becomes part of your stack.

9.1AUC: can the model rank?

AUC (area under the ROC curve) has a definition that sounds scary and a meaning that's actually simple. Pick a random clicked impression and a random non-clicked one. AUC is the chance the clicked one scores higher, with half credit for a tie. A coin flip gets about 0.5. A perfect ranking of the observed labels gets 1.0. Even scoring with Pebble's true click probabilities usually falls short, because clicks are random. On a finite sample, a learned model can happen to beat that truth score; treat it as a benchmark, not an inviolable ceiling.

AUC ignores calibration (only the order matters), and changing the positive/negative mix alone does not change it if their score distributions stay the same. A traffic shift can change those distributions and AUC. It also averages over every positive-negative pair, including pairs the auction never compares, like different users at different times.

9.2Normalized entropy: are the numbers right?

Log loss measures how good the probabilities are, but raw values are hard to compare across datasets with different base rates. The constant predictor for a 1% CTR dataset has lower log loss than the constant predictor for a 10% dataset; a good model on the latter can still beat a bad model on the former. Normalized entropy (NE), popularized by Facebook's ads team (He et al., 2014), divides the model's log loss by the loss from always predicting that dataset's average CTR.

\[ \text{NE} = \frac{-\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \big[y_i \log \hat p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat p_i)\big]}{-\big[\bar y \log \bar y + (1-\bar y)\log(1-\bar y)\big]} \]

NE = 1 means "no better than just predicting the average." Lower is better. And because log loss punishes miscalibration, NE captures ranking and calibration together. That makes it the natural headline metric for a model whose outputs literally become prices.

A dataset has a 10% click rate. Your model's average log loss on it is 0.29. What's its NE?

Hint

Denominator: −(0.1 ln 0.1 + 0.9 ln 0.9).

−(0.1 × −2.303 + 0.9 × −0.105) = 0.325, and 0.29 / 0.325 ≈ 0.892. The model removes about 11% of the uncertainty that predicting the average leaves on the table. For perspective: in mature ads systems, launches are often decided on NE improvements of a tenth of a percent. People throw parties over 0.1%.

Common trap

Evaluating on a random sample of the training period instead of a later one. Ads data drifts (Chapter 14) and has leaky time structure (Chapter 6), so a random split flatters the model, like grading a student on the exact homework problems they already did. Always evaluate on data from after the training window, the way the model will actually be used.

9.5The offline–online gap

Now for the plot twist. Offline evaluation has a blind spot baked right into it: it measures the model on impressions the old system chose to show. But once the new model is in charge, it'll be scoring ~150 candidates per request, and most of those the old system never showed. So there are no labels for them. None.

If the model is wildly wrong about those unseen candidates, offline metrics simply can't tell you. The only way to find out is to serve it. This is the offline–online gap, and its most common cause is our old friend from Chapter 5, exposure bias.

In real life you can't see this gap until it bites you. In the simulator, we can measure it directly: for held-out requests, compare each model's predictions against the truth on all eligible candidates, not just the ones that got shown.

9.6The A/B test

To estimate what a model does in production, randomly assign a slice of users to the current system (control) or the new one (treatment). Assignment by user ID keeps a user in one arm. Under valid randomization, comparable measurement and limited spillovers between arms, their average difference estimates the model's effect. They share a calendar and marketplace, but finite groups can still differ by chance; shared budgets can also make one arm affect the other.

"Bigger than chance" is the hard part. Revenue per user is noisy: most users click nothing, and a few click a lot. The widget reports a 95% confidence interval for each observed lift, computed by resampling users. An interval containing zero is inconclusive about the sign, not evidence of no harm. The simulator also shows an oracle diagnostic based on expected outcomes. That removes click and conversion sampling noise, but differences between assigned users can remain.

Run an experiment on your stack
Control: your heuristic. Treatment: your logistic regression. Three days, 4,000 requests a day, users split 50/50.
Going deeper: experiment preflight

Before ramping, decide the randomization unit, primary metric, smallest effect worth detecting, power and duration, and how long labels must mature. Set a tolerable loss margin for each guardrail; an interval that includes zero does not show a guardrail is safe. Check that assignment and analysis counts match the planned split (sample-ratio mismatch), then use predefined stop and ramp rules. If you inspect many metrics, account for multiple comparisons. Analyze users by assignment even if they saw no ad; exposed-only comparisons can reintroduce selection. Shared budgets can cause interference across arms.

Intuition

Try the A/A test button (both arms identical) and you'll still see "lifts" of several percent. From nothing! That's the noise floor, and a real effect has to climb over it. Real ads experiments run for weeks on millions of users to detect lifts of a fraction of a percent. Your three-day test on a few thousand simulated users can only catch the big stuff.

Your new model improves offline AUC from 0.742 to 0.748. In a two-week A/B test, revenue per user is −0.4% with a 95% CI of [−1.1%, +0.3%]. What's the right reading?

  • The model hurts revenue; roll it back
  • The model helps revenue
  • The result is inconclusive: it permits some harm and some gain. Check the predeclared revenue and guardrail margins before deciding
  • Ship it, since AUC is up

The interval allows a revenue loss as large as 1.1% or a gain as large as 0.3% at its endpoints. If your predeclared acceptable loss is smaller than 1.1%, this result cannot establish noninferiority. Check guardrails and whether the offline gain came from cross-user pairs the auction never compares; gather more data if the decision still matters.

An A/B test shows CTR +6% (significant) but advertiser conversions −3% (significant). What's the most likely story?

  • Measurement noise
  • The model favors ads that get clicked but don't convert, such as attention-grabbing creatives
  • Users clicked more, so they must have bought more
  • Advertisers ran out of budget

A click model optimizes clicks. That's all it knows how to do. If the ads that gain delivery are clickbait-y (high CTR, low CVR), clicks go up while conversions go down. Advertisers end up paying more per conversion, and eventually they bid less. That's why advertiser value is a guardrail in ads experiments, and why Chapter 12 adds a conversion model.

What you built

An evaluation toolkit: your own AUC and NE, reliability diagrams and slices, and a real A/B testing harness with confidence intervals. And with it, you made your stack's very first launch decision. Put that on your resume.

Here's the thing, though. Your stack is still scoring every eligible ad for every request. That works when there are 340 ads. It does not work when there are three million. Next time, we fix that, by building a model whose whole job is to find a handful of needles in a very large haystack, very, very fast.
