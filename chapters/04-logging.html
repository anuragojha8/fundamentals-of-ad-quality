Chapter 4 · Part II · Data

Logging: The Memory of the System

In which I learn that nothing happened unless somebody wrote it down (correctly, at the right time).

Here's an awkward confession about your stack: it has the memory of a goldfish. It's been happily serving ads for three chapters, and every single request vanished the moment it was served. Poof. Gone. So before we can train even one model, we need logs.

I'll be upfront: this is the least glamorous chapter in the book. Nobody ever got a standing ovation for a join. But it might be the most important one. When an ML team says "our model got worse and we don't know why," I'd bet you a nice dinner the answer is hiding in the logs.

4.1What gets written down

An ad system doesn't keep one tidy diary. It writes several separate streams of records, at different times, from different machines:

  • The impression log, written by the ad server at serve time: request id, timestamp, user and context, which ad was shown in which slot, the bid, the prediction the model made, the price, the model version, and a snapshot of the feature values the model used.
  • The click log, written seconds or minutes later, when the user taps. It records the request id and ad id, so it can be matched back to its impression.
  • The conversion log, written hours or even days later, usually reported by the advertiser's website or app through a pixel or a server-to-server API. It's often late, sometimes duplicated, and occasionally lost forever, like a sock in the dryer.

Training data gets made by joining these streams. Each impression becomes one training row, and its label answers one question: "was there a matching click (or conversion) inside the allowed window?"

4.2Build: the label join

The join quietly decides what a positive example is, which makes it a much bigger deal than it looks. Three rules matter:

  1. Match on the right key. One request shows three ads, so the key is (request id, ad id), not just the request id.
  2. Windows. A click counts if it happens within the click window after the impression (say, one hour). A conversion counts if it happens within the attribution window (say, 7 days) and follows a click.
  3. Now. An event that hasn't happened by the time the join runs doesn't exist yet. Your function gets nowT and has to ignore anything after it. That's exactly how the real pipeline behaves, and it's going to matter a lot in Chapter 12.
Common trap

Joining on request id alone. With three ads per request, one click on the slot-2 ad also labels slots 1 and 3 as clicked, tripling your "positives" with pure noise. And here's the evil part: this kind of bug doesn't crash anything. Metrics just get a little bit worse, and nobody figures out why for a whole quarter. Label pipelines deserve unit tests, just like the ones you just passed.

4.3Windows and "now"

Conversions take their sweet time. For a mobile game, they show up in minutes. For a bank account, they can take days. So if the training job runs right at the end of day 9, the impressions from day 9 haven't had any time to convert, and they get labeled 0, even though some of them are going to convert tomorrow. Intuition

Every label is a statement made at a particular moment in time. "Didn't convert" really means "hadn't converted yet when we checked." And here's the cruel irony: fresh data is the most valuable data for a model, because it's closest to today's world. It's also the data with the most wrong labels. Chapter 12 is all about living with that.

Your pCVR model trains every morning on the last 14 days of impressions, joined at 6 am. Nimbus (a bank, where conversions take days) complains that the model systematically under-predicts its conversions. Gaming advertisers don't see this. What's the most likely cause?

  • Nimbus's creatives are low quality
  • Recent Nimbus impressions haven't had time to convert when the join runs, so they're labeled as negatives
  • The click window is too short
  • Feature hashing collisions

With slow conversions, a big chunk of the last few days' eventual conversions hasn't arrived when the join runs. Those rows become false negatives, and the model learns that Nimbus converts less than it really does. Gaming conversions show up within hours, so they barely notice. This is called delayed feedback. The fixes: wait longer before using data (and lose freshness), reweight, or model the delay explicitly (Chapter 12).

4.4Log features at serve time

Here's a question you'll definitely hear in a meeting someday: why bother storing feature values in the impression log at all? Why not just recompute them at training time from the raw data? It's cheaper!

Because features change. Take the counting feature you'll build in Chapter 6: an ad's historical CTR. At serve time on day 3, the model saw that ad's CTR as of day 3. Recompute it at training time on day 10 and you get the CTR as of day 10, which includes clicks that happened after the impression. That's information from the future. Your model is basically reading tomorrow's newspaper.

Training on recomputed features creates train/serve skew: the model learns from feature values it'll never see in production. Worse, because those recomputed values include the outcome period, they leak the label. The model looks amazing offline and disappoints online. Let's see how big the difference is in your logs.

In your job

Serve-time feature logging is expensive, because it multiplies log volume. So there's constant pressure to cut it ("we can just recompute, it's fine"). Push back with the skew argument, or at the very least insist on measuring the skew before anyone flips a switch. The same logic applies to everything else you log: the model version (so you can tell which model did what), the candidates that lost (so you can study what you didn't show), and exploration flags and probabilities (Chapter 5). Data you didn't log is data you can never, ever learn from.

4.5Downsampling, and undoing it

Real impression logs are enormous, and most of the rows are negatives: people scrolling right past. So training pipelines usually keep every positive but only a fraction \(w\) of the negatives. It's way cheaper, and it barely hurts ranking.

But there's a catch (there's always a catch). A model trained on downsampled data predicts on the downsampled scale, so its probabilities come out too high. You undo it with a correction that every ads engineer knows by heart, and that you should recognize when it pops up in a design doc:

\[ q = \frac{p}{p + (1-p)/w} \]

where \(p\) is the model's raw prediction and \(q\) is the corrected probability on real traffic.

Your pCTR model was trained keeping all clicks but only 10% of non-clicks (\(w = 0.1\)). For some impression it outputs \(p = 0.2\). What's the calibrated CTR \(q\)? (Answer as a probability, e.g. 0.05.)

Hint

(1 − p)/w = 0.8 / 0.1 = 8.

q = 0.2 / (0.2 + 8) ≈ 0.0244, or 2.4%. Forget this correction and your model looks 8× over-calibrated. It's a surprisingly common launch bug, and it's exactly why calibration gets checked on unsampled evaluation data.

Which of these does not need to be in the impression log for your stack to learn well?

  • The pCTR the model predicted at serve time
  • The slot the ad was shown in
  • The user's click, if any
  • The feature values used by the model

Trick question, sort of. The click hasn't happened yet at serve time! It lives in its own event stream and gets joined later. Everything else on the list has to be captured at serve time, because you can't faithfully reconstruct it afterwards.

Infra proposes cutting storage costs 40% by (a) no longer logging feature values and recomputing them at training time, and (b) keeping only 5% of non-click impressions. How do you evaluate the proposal?

  • (a) is risky, and it's measurable. Ask for a skew analysis: for a sample of logged impressions, compare logged and recomputed values feature by feature. Which ones drift (counting features, anything time-windowed, anything mutable like account status)? Then train on recomputed features and evaluate on logged ones. That AUC gap is your skew cost. A middle ground: only log the features that can't be faithfully recomputed.
  • (b) is usually fine for ranking, if done right. Check that the correction \(q = p/(p+(1-p)/w)\) is applied in serving, that evaluation uses unsampled data, and that rare segments (new advertisers, small countries) keep enough negatives to stay calibrated. Consider segment-aware sampling rates.
  • Don't lose the ability to learn later. Keep a small, unsampled, fully logged holdout stream for audits, research, and future models.
  • Put numbers on both sides: the storage savings vs. the expected metric loss, converted to revenue.

What you built

Your stack finally has a memory. It keeps logs: impression records with serve-time features and predictions, plus separate click and conversion streams. And your join turns them into labeled training rows.

So we're done, right? We've got labels, let's train! Well… not so fast. Those labels are honest about what happened, but they're keeping a few secrets about why it happened. Next time, we interrogate them.
