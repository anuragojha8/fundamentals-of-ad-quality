How a Model Learns

In which I walk downhill 360,000 times and call it artificial intelligence.

Okay, this is the big one. We have logs (Chapter 4), labels whose little lies we understand (Chapter 5), and features (Chapter 6). Today we train an actual model.

And I have some good news for you: the heart of machine learning fits in about eight lines of code. Predict something. Measure how wrong you were. Nudge every weight a tiny bit in the direction that would've helped. Repeat 360,000 times. That's it. That's the secret. You're going to write that loop yourself, and train a logistic regression on your own logs, right here in the browser. It's the same model family that ran most of the world's ad auctions for about a decade, and it's still the baseline every fancy new model has to beat.

7.1The model

Logistic regression computes a score \(z\), called the logit, by adding up one weight per active feature. Then it squashes that score into a probability with the sigmoid:

\[ z = \sum_{f \in \text{features}(x)} w_f, \qquad \hat p = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} \]

The nice thing is that every weight means something you can read. It's how much that feature shifts the log-odds of a click. A weight of +0.7 on hour×cat=12_Food means "Food ads at noon: roughly double the odds," since \(e^{0.7} \approx 2\). Training just means finding the weights that make the predictions match the labels as closely as possible.

7.2Measuring wrongness: log loss

"As closely as possible" needs to be an actual number, or the computer gets confused. For probabilities, the standard choice is log loss (also called binary cross-entropy):

\[ \ell(\hat p, y) = -\big[\, y \log \hat p + (1-y)\log(1-\hat p) \,\big] \]

If the user clicked (\(y = 1\)), the loss is \(-\log \hat p\): small if you predicted a high probability, huge if you predicted something close to zero. It punishes confident mistakes the hardest, which, honestly, is fair. Chapter 8 compares it with other losses. For now, one fact matters, and it's the reason logistic regression is so pleasant to train. The derivative of log loss with respect to the logit is just

\[ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z} = \hat p - y \]

the prediction minus the truth. That's it. And since \(z\) is a sum of weights, every active weight gets that same gradient. So one training step is:

\[ w_f \leftarrow w_f - \eta \,(\hat p - y) \quad \text{for every active feature } f \]

where \(\eta\) is the learning rate.

Let's sanity-check it. If the model under-predicted a click (\(\hat p = 0.02\), \(y=1\)), every active weight goes up by \(0.98\eta\). Big nudge. If it predicted a non-click at 2%, every active weight goes down by \(0.02\eta\). Tiny nudge. Lots of small downward nudges from non-clicks balance out a few big upward ones from clicks, and the weights settle exactly where the predicted rates match the observed ones. Pretty elegant, right?

Intuition

The learning rate is just a step size. Too small and training crawls; you run out of data before you get anywhere. Too big and every step overshoots the valley floor, bouncing from wall to wall like a pinball, or flying off into the void entirely. Real training uses stochastic gradient descent: one example (or a small batch) per step. Each step is noisy and a little drunk, but you take millions of them, and on average they head downhill.

A feature's weight is 0.5. On an example where that feature is active, the model predicts \(\hat p = 0.2\), and the user clicked (\(y = 1\)). With learning rate 0.1 and no regularization, what's the weight after this step?

Hint

Gradient = p̂ − y = −0.8. New weight = old − η × gradient.

0.5 − 0.1 × (0.2 − 1) = 0.5 + 0.08 = 0.58. The click pulled the weight up, so next time this feature shows up, the prediction will be a little higher. Congratulations, you just did machine learning by hand.

Common trap

More epochs isn't more learning. Go ahead, try 5 epochs above. Training loss keeps dropping while held-out loss flattens out or even climbs. What's happening is the model is memorizing rare features, like the IDs of users with only a handful of impressions, instead of learning patterns that carry over. Big ads models are famous for this. Deep CTR models often overfit after just one epoch, which is why lots of production systems train on each example exactly once (Zhang et al., "Towards Understanding the Overfitting Phenomenon of Deep Click-Through Rate Models," 2022).

7.6Overfitting and regularization

A model overfits when it fits noise in the training data that won't show up again. In ads, the usual suspects are rare IDs. A user with 8 impressions and 1 click gets a weight tuned perfectly to that one click, which tells you approximately nothing about their next click.

L2 regularization adds a penalty \(\lambda \sum w^2\) to the loss, which, in the update, turns into a constant pull toward zero. Weights for common features, backed by tons of data, shrug it off. Weights for rare features, backed by very little data, stay small. That's usually exactly what you want. Overdo it, though, and every weight stays small, and the model underfits: too timid to learn anything useful.

After training, held-out log loss is much worse than training log loss, and the gap grows each epoch. What's the best first move?

  • Train for more epochs
  • Stop earlier and/or increase regularization
  • Raise the learning rate
  • Evaluate on training data instead

A growing gap between training and held-out loss is the classic fingerprint of overfitting. Stop at the epoch where held-out loss was lowest (early stopping), regularize more, or cut back the capacity that's doing the memorizing, e.g. by hashing rare IDs together or dropping them entirely.

Training loss goes up and down wildly and never settles; after a while the predictions are all 0 or 1. What's most likely wrong?

  • The learning rate is too high
  • Regularization is too strong
  • Too few epochs
  • Too many hash collisions

The pinball problem. Each step jumps past the minimum, the weights keep growing, and the sigmoid gets stuck at 0 or 1. Lower the learning rate, or use an adaptive optimizer that picks a step size per weight (AdaGrad, Adam).

Going deeper: optimizers, batches, and why ads models use AdaGrad

Your step uses one learning rate for every weight. That's a poor fit for sparse data: the bias feature shows up on every row and gets millions of updates, while a user ID shows up on a handful. AdaGrad gives each weight its own step size, which shrinks as that weight accumulates squared gradients, so rare features learn quickly and common ones settle down. For years it was the standard for sparse CTR models. The FTRL-Proximal algorithm from Google's "Ad Click Prediction: a View from the Trenches" (McMahan et al., 2013) is a close cousin that also produces sparse weights. Deep models use Adam for dense layers and often AdaGrad-style updates for embedding tables. Mini-batching (averaging gradients over, say, 512 examples per step) makes each step less noisy and lets GPUs chew through examples in parallel.

An engineer reports: "The new model's training loss is 8% lower than the current model's." Is that good news? What would you ask?

  • Training loss isn't the goal. A model can crush training loss just by memorizing. Ask for held-out metrics on data from after the training period (not a random split, which leaks the future into training), plus calibration.
  • Same data? Were both models trained and evaluated on the same examples, with the same label definition and the same sampling? If not, the numbers aren't comparable.
  • How many epochs, and where was held-out loss lowest? Falling training loss with rising held-out loss means overfitting.
  • Where does it help? Slices by new vs. established ads and users, and by country.
  • And ultimately: the online A/B test (Chapter 9).
