How to Read This Book

In which I become an Ads Quality PM, and nobody warns me about the math.

Look, I'll be honest with you. Most PMs who work on ads know the words. pCTR. eCPM. Two-tower. Calibration. You nod along in the model review, you write "calibration ↑" in your notes, and you hope nobody asks you a follow-up question. I've been there. It's not a great feeling.

Far fewer people can explain why a model that improved AUC made revenue go down, or why the ML team wants to spend an entire quarter on, of all things, logging. That second kind of knowledge is what this book is about. An ad system is a machine that turns predictions into prices. Once you understand the predictions, you understand the business.

This book is for product managers with a computer science background. You've taken a probability course, you can read Python, and you remember roughly what a derivative is (it's the slope thing, don't panic). You don't need to have trained a model. By the end, I promise you'll be able to:

  • Trace an ad request end to end, from the moment a user opens the app to the moment a conversion comes back and becomes a training label.
  • Reason about the auction: how predictions set prices, and why a miscalibrated model is a pricing bug.
  • Read a model design doc and ask good questions about its labels, features, loss, architecture, and evaluation.
  • Tell a real win from a measurement artifact in an offline metric or an A/B test.
  • Make the tradeoffs a quality PM owns: revenue vs. user experience, latency vs. model size, freshness vs. cost, short-term vs. long-term.

0.2Our running example: Pebble

The setup

Congratulations! You've just joined Pebble, a photo-sharing app with 80 million daily users, as the PM for Ads Quality. Pebble shows one ad for roughly every eight posts in the feed, and about 40,000 advertisers fight over those slots. Your team owns the models that decide which ad a user sees and how much the advertiser pays. So, you know. Just the money.

You'll meet a few Pebble advertisers along the way: Trailhead (hiking boots), Nimbus (a neobank), Sprout (meal kits), Orbit (a mobile game), and Lumen (skincare). They all want different things, and at some point every one of them will be mad at you. That's how you know you're doing the job.

Pebble is made up (sadly, I don't own a photo app with 80 million users), but its numbers are realistic in shape, and every mechanism in the book is one that real platforms actually use. Where a technique comes from a specific published paper, I'll name it so you can go read the original if you're into that kind of thing.

0.3What you'll build

Here's the fun part. This isn't a book you just read. Chapter by chapter, we're going to build one working ad system together, running entirely in your browser, against a simulated Pebble: 4,000 users, 60 advertisers, and about 340 ads, all clicking, converting, and occasionally rage-hiding ads in realistic ways. You'll write the key pieces yourself in small JavaScript functions: the eligibility filter, the auction, the label join, the gradient step, the evaluation metrics. You'll train real models on your own logs and ship them into your own stack. Pretty cool, right?

Two things make the simulation a teaching tool rather than a toy:

  • It knows the truth. Every user–ad pair has a true click probability, a true conversion probability, and a true hide probability. Your models never get to see them, but the widgets do, and they use them to grade your models against reality instead of against noisy logged labels. At work you will never, ever get this. Here, you get to see exactly what your metrics were hiding from you.
  • Your decisions stick. The exploration rate you pick in Chapter 5 shapes the logs your Chapter 7 model learns from, which decides whether it wins the Chapter 9 A/B test. (Yes, I'm setting you up. No, I won't tell you the right answer yet.) Your code, settings, and trained models are saved in this browser.
Skipping ahead?

No judgment. Every chapter starts with a Your ad stack panel. If you jump to a chapter whose prerequisites you haven't built, a Load reference solutions button fills them in, trains any missing models, and lets you pick up from there. Anything you built yourself stays yours. Every code exercise has a reference solution too, so nothing can block you.

Every chapter mixes a few kinds of material, and each looks different so you can skim or go deep depending on how much coffee you've had:

Going deeper

Optional detail for the curious: derivations, paper references, edge cases. Skip them guilt-free. The story still makes sense without them.

And then there's the stuff you get to poke at:

  • Build steps (marked BUILD). A small function to write, with tests that run in the page. Your code joins your stack as soon as its tests pass; until then, the stack uses the reference solution.
  • Simulations (marked INTERACTIVE). Most run on your own stack. Drag things. Try to break them. Honestly, breaking them is where most of the learning happens.
  • Checks: multiple-choice questions and numeric exercises, graded in the page. A chapter counts as complete when you've solved all of them.
  • Apply it: open-ended PM scenarios. Write your answer, then compare it with a strong one. They don't count toward completion, but between you and me, they're the most useful part of the book.

0.5A note on math (and code)

The code exercises are short, usually 5 to 15 lines of plain JavaScript, and each one starts from a commented template. If you can read Python, you can write these. I believe in you.

There's some math too. Not much, and it's always the kind that tells you something. You'll see probabilities like \(P(\text{click} \mid \text{user}, \text{ad})\), the logistic function, a few sums, and one or two gradients. Whenever a formula shows up, the sentence right after it says what it means in plain English. If you can read this one, you're ready:

\[ \mathbb{E}[\text{revenue per impression}] = P(\text{click}) \times \text{price per click} \]

Warm-up. Trailhead pays $2.00 per click, and its ad is clicked on 1.5% of impressions. What's the expected revenue from showing it 1,000 times?

  • $2.00
  • $30.00
  • $300.00
  • $15.00

1,000 × 0.015 = 15 expected clicks, and 15 × $2.00 = $30.00. Revenue per thousand impressions has a name, eCPM (effective cost per mille), and it's the single most important number in this book. It'll be back in Chapter 2. It's always back.

Before you move on

Ad systems are a loop: show → log → learn → show. Predictions (the probability of a click, a conversion, a hide) feed the auction, and the auction turns them into prices. In the rest of the book, you build every box yourself.
