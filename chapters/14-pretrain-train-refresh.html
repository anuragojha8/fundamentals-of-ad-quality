Chapter 14 · Part IV · The Models

Pre-training, Fine-tuning, and Freshness

In which the world moves on and my model doesn't notice.

You know how you find an old photo of yourself and think, "who is that?" Models have the same problem, except they don't know it.

Every model in your stack was trained on days 0 through 9. But Pebble kept going after day 9. Users' interests wander. Seasons change what people click on. Advertisers launch new creatives every day that your model has literally never seen. A model is just a snapshot of a world that refuses to hold still, and keeping it current matters just as much as how cleverly you built it. In this chapter we measure how fast your model goes stale, design a retraining schedule, and meet the ideas modern systems use to learn faster from less: warm starts, pre-training, and distillation.

14.1Drift: watching a model go stale

Pebble's simulated world drifts in three ways, just like the real one. Each user's interests slowly shift toward a different set of favorite categories over the 90 days (the hiking person gets really into cooking, it happens). Categories have seasonal trends (Travel rises and falls over weeks). And about 40% of ads launch after day 0, so a model trained early has never even heard of them. Staleness hurts in two ways. Ranking decays as the patterns it learned become less true. A user ID's weight describes who that user used to be, and new ads don't have an ID weight at all. Calibration wanders as the mix of users, ads, and seasons shifts around, sometimes up, sometimes down, which makes it really easy to miss on an aggregate dashboard. That's why production systems pair a periodic full retrain with a calibration layer that gets refreshed often (Chapter 3 to the rescue, again).

14.2Designing a retraining schedule

So how often should you retrain? Retrain every hour and the model stays fresh, but you pay in compute, pipeline complexity, and risk (every retrain is a brand-new model that could break something). Retrain once a month and it's cheap, and also stale. And there are two more choices hiding in there:

  • Cold start retraining: train from scratch on a recent window (say, the last 10 days) every time.
  • Warm start (incremental) training: start from the previous model's weights and train only on the data that's arrived since. Much cheaper per update, and it carries forward everything the model already knew. The risk is that it also carries forward stale knowledge, or bugs.

Lots of large systems train continuously: a warm-started model eats new logs as they arrive (that's online learning), with an occasional cold rebuild to reset whatever drift has piled up.

Validate it on fresh data and monitor auction outcomes; a flexible histogram mapping can even change ranking unless constrained to be monotone.

14.3Pre-training and fine-tuning

Here's something we've been dancing around for a few chapters. Ad clicks are a thin, biased signal: rare, and only available for ads that got shown (Chapters 5 and 10). But Pebble knows so much more about its users than their ad clicks. Every like, comment, follow, and search is a clue about what they're into.

Pre-training means learning general representations from all those abundant signals first, and then fine-tuning on the scarce task you actually care about:

If this sounds familiar, it should. It's the same idea behind large language models, which get pre-trained on the whole web and then fine-tuned for specific tasks. In ads, "foundation" user models pre-trained on engagement sequences, and content embeddings from image and text encoders, increasingly get shared across lots of ranking models. A brand-new ad has zero click history, but its image and text have an embedding from day one. A brand-new user has zero ad clicks, but they've already liked a bunch of posts. So the biggest wins land exactly where it hurts most: cold start and sparse labels.

14.4Distillation

Sometimes your best model is just too expensive to run at every stage of the funnel. Knowledge distillation trains a small "student" model to imitate a big "teacher." Instead of learning from 0/1 labels, the student learns from the teacher's predicted probabilities, which are way more informative per example. A click label just says "0." The teacher says "this was a 7% case, not a 0.1% case," which tells you a whole lot more.

In ads, a common use is having a heavy late-stage ranker teach the early-stage ranker, so the early stage agrees more with the late one and the eventual winners survive the funnel more often (hi again, Chapter 1). It's also how a slow offline model can teach a fast online one.

Why do soft labels from a teacher help a student more than the original 0/1 click labels?

  • The teacher sees more examples
  • A probability like 0.07 tells the student how likely a click was; a 0 only says it didn't happen this time
  • The teacher is always perfectly calibrated
  • The student must have the same architecture

Clicks are noisy coin flips drawn from probabilities. The teacher's estimate strips out a lot of that noise, the same way "expected clicks" did all through this book. So the student picks up the teacher's knowledge at a fraction of the data cost.

Infra says moving your ranking model from daily to hourly retraining will cost an extra 30% in training compute. How would you decide whether it's worth it?

  • Measure the value of freshness directly: evaluate models of different ages on the same recent traffic (like the widget above). How much AUC and calibration does a 23-hour-older model lose? Is the loss concentrated where freshness matters most: new ads, trending events, launch days for big campaigns?
  • Try cheaper alternatives first: hourly calibration refits, faster counting features (real-time ad CTR), and warm-start incremental updates instead of full retrains.
  • Translate it into money: use the NE-to-revenue relationship from past launches, and compare the expected gain with 30% of the training bill.
  • Risk: more frequent deployments need automated validation gates (calibration, NE, slice checks) before each model goes live. Otherwise an hourly pipeline can ship an hourly bug.
