Chapter 13 · Part IV · The Models

Privacy and Measurement

In which the conversions stop telling me who they were.

Chapter 12 handed you a conversion model, and I let you believe something generous: that every conversion comes back to you as a tidy little record saying this person, this ad, this moment. For most of the 2010s, that was roughly true.

It isn't anymore. Phones ask users whether an app may track them, and most say no. Browsers have been cutting back third-party cookies for years. Privacy laws mean a slice of your users never consented to measurement in the first place. And where conversion data still arrives, it increasingly arrives aggregated: counts per campaign per day rather than per person, delayed on purpose, with a bit of deliberate noise mixed in, and with small numbers withheld entirely.

So this chapter is about doing your job when some conversion data disappears and some arrives only as coarse totals. You'll measure what missing conversions do to your pCVR model, build an aggregate report with small-cell suppression, and see what happens to the numbers. The exercise illustrates a reporting tradeoff; it does not establish a privacy guarantee.

13.1Three ways the data gets worse

It helps to separate three things that often get lumped together as "privacy killed our measurement," because they break different parts of your stack:

  • Fewer events. Some conversions simply never reach you. The user opted out of tracking, declined consent, or is on a browser that blocks the mechanism you used to rely on. Your logs aren't wrong, they're incomplete, and they're incomplete unevenly: opt-in rates differ by platform, country, and app.
  • Coarser events. What does arrive may carry no user identifier, a coarse timestamp, or a conversion "value" squeezed into a handful of buckets. Apple's SKAdNetwork and AdAttributionKit use campaign-level postbacks. Google's Attribution Reporting API was another example, but Google announced its retirement in October 2025.
  • Aggregate controls. A system may suppress small cells and add noise. Our toy threshold counts conversions, not distinct people, so it is not k-anonymity. Neither suppression nor arbitrary noise alone proves a report safe to publish.

13.2What missing conversions do to your model

Start with the simulator's simplest version: each conversion event is independently observed with a chosen probability, and the rest silently never arrive. Your join (Chapter 4) labels those rows 0, exactly like the delayed ones in Chapter 12, except these never mature. Real consent is rarely this uniform.

Before you run it, predict what happens. Two things could break: the model's numbers (it should under-predict, since it sees fewer positives) and its ranking (it has less signal to learn from). Which one breaks first? Which one can you fix without any new data?

Intuition

Missing conversions hurt in two different ways. The level: under the toy's uniform observation assumption, a known event-observation probability lets you rescale average predictions in expectation, keeping each probability within 0–1. If observation varies by segment or depends on hidden outcomes, that shortcut fails. The signal: a model trained on fewer positives has less evidence about which user–ad pairs convert, and no rescaling restores it.

Common trap

Equating user opt-in rate with conversion-event observation rate. They differ when users convert at different rates or contribute multiple events. Observation also varies by platform, country, app version and vertical. A global correction may get one total close while miscalibrating segments, shifting auction outcomes. Estimate event observation by segment where the data supports it, and carry the uncertainty.

13.3Build: an aggregate report with suppression

Now the other direction. Instead of asking what you lose when events vanish, let's build one simple reporting control and look at what it costs.

The rule is simple enough to write in a few lines: group conversions into cells (here, one per advertiser per day), count them, and suppress cells with fewer than k conversions. One person could contribute several conversions; a count threshold does not tell us how many people are in a cell. Even whether a cell appears can reveal information.

The widget adds random noise after suppression to show its effect on utility. That arbitrary noise is not a differential-privacy mechanism. A real release needs a defined threat model and controls on contribution, queries and access; suppression boundaries can still leak information.

Tune both below, then look at who pays for them. The third dial is the one platforms actually reach for when advertisers complain: group more days into each cell. Bigger cells clear the threshold, so the suppressed advertisers reappear, and the price is reporting that arrives days late.

In your job

Notice who absorbs the damage: small advertisers. A big spender's daily cells clear any threshold and swamp the noise, so their reports look fine. A local business with four conversions a day may see nothing at all, forever, which means no conversion data, a worse pCVR model, worse delivery, and an account manager who can't explain why. When a privacy change ships, ask for the metric impact split by advertiser size. The average will look survivable and the bottom decile usually won't.

13.4Modeled conversions

So you have a model that sees too few conversions, and reports that are coarse, delayed, and noisy. The industry's answer, and you should know its name because you'll hear it in every review, is modeled conversions: use the data you do have (consented users, aggregate totals, your own experiments) to estimate the conversions you can't observe, then report and optimize against the estimate.

In practice that means a few things stacked together:

  • Scale to an available aggregate. A released cell is noisy and may omit events; suppressed cells give no count. Where an aggregate is usable, fit your model on observed data and calibrate totals with uncertainty, rather than treating the report as ground truth.
  • Learn from the consented slice, with assumptions. Consent selection differs from click selection. ESMM addresses the latter in logged impressions; it does not recover missing consent labels. Reweighting requires measured observation probabilities and overlap, and cannot repair missing features or populations with no labels.
  • Lean on experiments. When per-user attribution is impossible, randomized geo or user holdouts still measure the effect of ads, because they compare groups rather than individuals. Privacy takes away the log line. It doesn't take away the experiment.
Common trap

Treating modeled conversions as though they were counted ones. They carry uncertainty, and that uncertainty widens exactly where data is thinnest: new advertisers, small campaigns, rare verticals. Ask for confidence intervals on modeled numbers, and never put a modeled conversion into a billing calculation without knowing how far off it can be.

Going deeper: differential privacy in one paragraph

Differential privacy bounds how much the distribution of a released result changes between neighboring datasets that differ in one person's data. To make that bound meaningful, define the neighboring datasets, cap each person's contributions, calculate the query's sensitivity, and calibrate noise to that sensitivity and a chosen ε. Repeated releases compose and consume a privacy budget. Smaller ε generally means stronger bounds and less precise answers. It does not make a person unknowable or, by itself, settle whether a release complies with policy or law. Pebble's widget implements none of these formal steps. See Dwork and Roth for the formal treatment.

In this toy, each conversion is observed independently with probability 0.4. Your pCVR model treats unobserved conversions as 0. You divide every prediction by 0.4. What can that correct, and what remains broken?

  • It fixes the ranking of ads, but the average is still wrong
  • Under uniform missingness it can correct the average level (after keeping probabilities within 0–1), but it cannot restore lost ranking information
  • It fixes both: the model is as good as before
  • It fixes neither

With independent, uniformly missing conversions and a known observation probability, dividing by 0.4 can restore the overall level in expectation. Predictions must stay within 0–1. A constant multiplier cannot recover lost signal or change ranking. Real consent selection is often uneven, so a global correction can miscalibrate segments and shift auction outcomes.

Your toy report suppresses cells with fewer than 50 conversions. Which advertiser loses the most reported cells?

  • A national retailer with thousands of conversions a day
  • A small advertiser with a handful of conversions a day, whose cells are suppressed almost every day
  • Whoever bids on CPM
  • Whoever has the fastest conversions

Thresholds are flat, but advertisers aren't. Below the threshold you get nothing, so the smallest advertisers lose their conversion data entirely, and every downstream system that depends on it degrades for them first. The usual mitigations are wider cells (longer windows, campaigns grouped together), which trade timeliness for visibility, and modeled conversions for the cells that stay dark.

A platform change means only a third of Pebble's conversions will be observable next quarter, and the rest arrive as daily aggregates with a k-threshold. Write the plan you'd bring to your team: what breaks, what you'd measure, and what you'd tell advertisers.

  • What may break: in the toy with uniformly missing events, measured pCVR drops toward the observable share, which can lower target-CPA scores and delivery; ranking may degrade as positives get scarce; small advertisers lose report cells; and models trained on those labels inherit the measurement problem. With selective consent, the direction and size can differ by segment.
  • Measure first: the observable share by segment (platform, country, vertical, advertiser size), not globally. Then simulate the loss the way the widget above does: retrain on a thinned sample and grade against your best available truth. That gives you a number to plan around before the change lands.
  • Mitigations: test whether per-segment observation probabilities and overlap support reweighting; calibrate cautiously to usable noisy aggregates; use longer training windows where labels mature; estimate suppressed cells with explicit uncertainty; use randomized holdouts where consent and design allow.
  • Tell advertisers early and specifically: which metrics change definition (counted vs. modeled), when, and with what uncertainty. A reported number that quietly changes meaning is how you lose trust. Also expect reported conversions to fall short of the advertiser's own analytics, and prepare the reconciliation guidance before the escalations arrive.
  • Guardrails: watch CPA adherence and delivery for small advertisers specifically; keep a holdout where you can still measure end to end, if consent allows; and set a review date, because opt-in rates move.

What you built

An aggregate conversion report with suppression: conversions counted into cells, small cells omitted, and illustrative noise added by the widget. You measured the cost to reporting and model quality. This exercise does not validate a privacy mechanism.

Here's the uncomfortable part. Every model in your stack was trained on days 0 through 9, and Pebble didn't stop on day 9. Users' tastes are shifting, new ads launch daily, and your models are quietly going stale, with or without privacy. Next time, we find out how fast, and what to do about it.

Opt-in / consent rateThe share of users who consent; distinct from the share of conversion events observable, which can vary by segment and time.
Aggregate reportingCounts per campaign per period instead of per-person records.
Conversion-count suppressionOmit cells below a conversion threshold; this is not a distinct-person guarantee.
Differential privacyA bound on how much one person's data can change release probabilities, with bounded contributions, calibrated noise and composition.
