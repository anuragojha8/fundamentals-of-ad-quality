Chapter 15 · Part V · The Job

Quality Beyond the Click

In which I remember that nobody opened Pebble to see ads. (Shocking, I know.)

Let's take stock. For thirteen chapters, we've optimized clicks, conversions, and revenue. Those are what advertisers pay for, and you can measure them within a day. Very satisfying.

But here's the part nobody puts on the dashboard: ads have a cost that never shows up in the auction. Every irrelevant or annoying ad makes Pebble a tiny bit worse. People hide ads. They scroll faster. They open the app a little less often. Those effects are slow, spread thin, and super easy to ignore, right up until the day they aren't. Pebble's users didn't open the app to see ads. Every ad spends a little of their patience. The real job of a quality PM is to spend it wisely. So in this chapter we finally put users into your stack's objective.

15.1The objective you actually own

A healthy ads marketplace keeps three parties reasonably happy: advertisers (value for their money), users (a good experience), and the platform (revenue, both now and later). Pebble uses a simplified total value score. Both terms have units of dollars per impression:

\[ \text{score (\$/impression)} = \underbrace{\text{CPC bid (\$/click)} \times \text{pCTR}}_{\text{bid-weighted value}} \;-\; \underbrace{\lambda\;(\$/\text{hide}) \times \text{pHide}}_{\text{user-cost penalty}} \]

The weight \(\lambda\) prices a predicted hide in dollars. How many ad slots to show (ad load) is a separate allocation constraint, outside the score. Both are policy choices about user experience and short-term auction value; click data alone cannot choose them. No pressure. (Okay, a lot of pressure.)

15.2Predicting annoyance

Pebble lets users hide an ad ("not interested"). Hides are the clearest negative signal you'll ever get: a user literally telling you "please stop." They're also rare, about one per 180 impressions in your logs. In Pebble's world, hides come mostly from ads that don't match the user's interests, and from "annoying" creatives, which some advertisers are just really, really good at making.

The good news is you already know how to build this. Train a pHide model with exactly the same recipe as your pCTR model: same features, same trainer, different label. Look at you, reusing infrastructure like a pro.

15.3Pricing annoyance into the auction

Now let's add the penalty. Each ad's score becomes bid × pCTR − λ · pHide. Your stack passes an adjusted score through the auction's pCTR input so it can change ranking and clearing prices. That adjusted number is an implementation device, not a calibrated click probability. Sweep λ and look at the frontier.

Intuition

Wait, revenue went up? Yep. The first bit of quality penalty is often free, or even better than free. Ads that people hide are usually ads they wouldn't have clicked anyway, so pushing them down makes room for ads that actually earn more. Turn λ up far enough, though, and the penalty starts kicking out ads that make real money and are only mildly annoying. That's where the genuine tradeoff begins, and where you, the PM, have to make a call.

15.4Ad load and the long run

And now, the biggest lever of all: how many ads to show. More slots means more revenue today, almost linearly. It's so tempting. The cost shows up later: users who see more ads (and more hideable ones) come back a little less often. And a little less often, compounded over a year, is a lot.

Measuring that in real life takes long-running holdout experiments: a small group of users kept on lower ad load for months, compared with everyone else. Pebble's simulator doesn't model user churn, so the widget below uses a simple toy model where all the assumptions are right there on the label. You pick how sensitive users are, which is exactly the number a long-term holdout would be trying to estimate.

Short-term revenue vs. a year
Revenue and hides per request come from your stack (one simulated day per ad load). User retention is a toy model: weekly user loss = sensitivity × (0.8% × ad slots + 60% × hides per request), against 1% weekly organic growth.
Common trap

Deciding ad load from short-term revenue alone. More slots can raise immediate revenue while changes in retention take longer to observe; the trade depends on fill, demand and user response. A short test may miss later costs. Longer holdouts, validated proxy metrics and explicit user-experience guardrails help make the decision.

Adding a hide penalty (λ = 1) raised revenue and reduced hides in your simulation. Why can that happen?

  • The penalty only changes prices
  • Ads likely to be hidden are often also unlikely to be clicked, so demoting them promotes ads that earn more
  • Advertisers raised their bids
  • Pebble showed more ads

When pCTR isn't perfect (and it never is), pHide carries extra information about relevance. So the first bit of quality penalty partly fixes ranking mistakes. The real tradeoff only shows up once the penalty starts removing ads that are genuinely profitable.

Finance proposes raising Pebble's ad load from 1 ad in 8 posts to 1 in 6, projecting +18% revenue. Write your response: what you'd need to see, how you'd test it, and what guardrails you'd set.

  • Expected vs. measured: +18% assumes the ads in the new slots make as much as the existing ones. They usually don't. Marginal slots sit lower in the feed, get less attention, and dilute advertiser value. Expect less.
  • The long-term cost: ask for evidence from past ad-load changes or existing long-term holdouts. What happened to sessions per user, retention, and time spent 8–12 weeks later? Translate that into revenue lost over a year, because fewer sessions means fewer ads, too.
  • Test design: a long-running holdout (months) at the higher load, plus a smaller first step (1 in 7). Measure user metrics weekly, not just revenue.
  • Guardrails: agree on stop conditions ahead of time, e.g. hide rate +X%, sessions −Y%, retention −Z%. Also consider quality-aware load: show extra ads only where high-quality candidates exist (and to users who tolerate them), rather than a flat increase for everyone.
  • Advertiser side: more inventory can push prices down (more supply, same demand), so revenue per ad falls. One more reason that +18% is optimistic.

What you built

A pHide model, a quality penalty in your auction with a λ you picked, and an ad-load decision made with an explicit long-term assumption. You now own the objective, not just the models.

You've now built every piece of an ad system, from a hand-written heuristic all the way to a retrained, calibrated, quality-aware, two-stage ranking stack with retrieval and conversion bidding. Before you take it out for a spin, there's one question we've dodged for fifteen chapters: your stack counts a lot of conversions, but how many of them did it actually cause? Next time, we find out, and it's going to cost us some revenue to do it.
