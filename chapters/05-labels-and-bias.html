Labels and the Biases Hiding in Them

In which the clicks lie to me, but only a little, and only in ways I caused myself.

In Chapter 4 you turned events into labels: 1 if clicked, 0 if not. Clean, simple, honest. It's really tempting to treat those labels as the ground truth about what users like.

Look, I hate to be the one to tell you this, but they aren't. A click is the product of two things: what the user wanted, and what your system did. Which ad it picked. Where it put it. And, sneakiest of all, what it never showed at all. This chapter is about pulling those two things apart.

5.1Choosing the label is a product decision

Before we even get to bias, there's a more basic choice: what are we predicting in the first place? "Click" is just one option, and every option quietly makes the whole system optimize for something different:

Label definitions are where product intent turns into math. "Should an accidental tap that bounces after one second count as a click?" sounds like a boring data-engineering detail. It's actually a decision about which advertisers win. So in every model review, ask what the label is. Then ask who signed off on it. You'll be surprised how often the answer is "uh…"

5.2Position bias

Here's something obvious once you say it out loud: people don't look at every slot. An ad in the third slot often gets scrolled right past before anyone reads it. The standard way to model this is the examination hypothesis. To click, a user first has to look at the ad, and then want it:

\[ P(\text{click} \mid \text{ad}, \text{slot}) = \underbrace{P(\text{examine} \mid \text{slot})}_{\text{position bias}} \times \underbrace{P(\text{click} \mid \text{examined}, \text{ad})}_{\text{relevance}} \]

The model's job is to estimate relevance, the second part. But the logs hand it both parts mixed together. And here's the trap: in your production logs, slot 1's CTR is way higher than slot 3's. How much of that is position, and how much is just that your stack put its best ads in slot 1? From the logs alone, you honestly can't tell. 5.3Randomize to measure

So how do we untangle them without a simulator? If I asked you to design an experiment for this, what would you do?

Here's the clean trick. On a small share of requests, keep the same three winners but shuffle their order. In shuffled traffic, every ad is equally likely to land in any slot, so relevance is the same across slots on average. Whatever CTR difference is left over has to be pure position:

\[ \widehat{\text{examine}}(s) = \frac{\text{CTR of slot } s \text{ in shuffled traffic}}{\text{CTR of slot 1 in shuffled traffic}} \]
Measure position bias with a shuffle experiment
Five days with 20% of requests shuffled. Compare the estimate from shuffled traffic with the naive one from normal traffic, and with the truth.
Intuition

Your logs can only answer one question: "what happened when we did what we always do?" To find out what would happen if you did something else, you have to occasionally, actually, do something else. On purpose, and in a way you can measure. That one idea is behind every randomized experiment, every exploration budget, and every A/B test in this book.

5.4Build: inverse propensity weighting

Once you know how often each slot gets looked at, you can correct an ad's CTR for where it was shown. Think about it this way: a click in slot 3, where only 41% of people even look, is stronger evidence than a click in slot 1. The user had to notice it first! So we weight each click by \(1/\text{examine}(s)\).

This is called inverse propensity weighting (IPW), and it's one of those ideas that shows up everywhere once you know it: weight each observation by one over the probability that the system let you observe it.

\[ \widehat{\text{CTR}}_{\text{IPW}} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{y_i}{\text{examine}(s_i)} \]

Orbit's ad was shown 100 times, all in slot 3, where users examine the slot 40% of the time. It got 2 clicks. What's its IPW-corrected CTR? (Answer as a probability.)

Hint

Each click counts as 1 / 0.4 = 2.5 clicks.

(2 × 2.5) / 100 = 0.05. The naive estimate of 2% undersells Orbit by 2.5×. And if your ranking used that naive number, Orbit would stay stuck in low slots forever, collecting low CTRs that "prove" it belongs in low slots. A self-fulfilling prophecy, in ad form.

Going deeper: position in the model itself

Production rankers usually handle position inside the model rather than through IPW. The slot is a feature during training, and it's pinned to a fixed value (slot 1) at serving time, so the model outputs relevance. Your Chapter 7 model does exactly this. A fancier version uses a separate shallow tower for position-like features, whose output is added to the main model's logit during training and dropped at serving. That keeps the main tower from confusing "shown high" with "good" (see the PAL paper from Huawei, 2019, and YouTube's multitask ranking paper, 2019). IPW is still the go-to tool for unbiased evaluation and for learning-to-rank losses.

5.5Selection bias and the feedback loop

Position bias messes with what you learn about ads you showed. But there's a much deeper problem: the ads you didn't show. For those, you have no labels at all. Zero.

Follow the logic. If the model underrates an ad, the ad doesn't get shown. If it doesn't get shown, no clicks come in. If no clicks come in, the model never finds out it was wrong. Forever. That's a feedback loop, and it's why every serious ad system keeps an exploration budget: a small slice of traffic where the system deliberately shows ads it isn't sure about.

Common trap

Judging exploration by this quarter's revenue. Exploration usually costs money in the short term, because you're deliberately showing ads you think are worse. The payoff is information, and it arrives later, when models get trained on logs that contain it. Short tests will always make "no exploration" look like the winner. You'll see the payoff (or the lack of it) on your own stack in Chapter 9.

5.6Your decision: exploration in production

Okay, decision time. Your stack's logs are about to become training data (Chapters 6 and 7). How much of Pebble's traffic should go to exploration from now on? This setting gets saved, and it shapes every log your stack collects from here on, including the ones your models learn from.

Choose wisely. There's no free option. (And no, I'm not going to tell you the answer. Future you will find out.)

Set production exploration
Explored requests show random eligible ads for free. They're flagged in the logs.

In your normal logs, slot 1's CTR is 2.8× slot 3's. A shuffle experiment shows users examine slot 3 about 41% as often as slot 1. What explains the rest of the gap?

  • Position bias is larger than the experiment suggests
  • Selection: the ranker puts more relevant ads in slot 1, so slot 1's ads are better on average
  • A logging bug in slot 3
  • Slot 3 is shown to different users

Observed CTR ratio ≈ examination ratio × relevance ratio. The shuffle gives examination ≈ 1/0.41 ≈ 2.4×, so the leftover ≈ 1.15× is selection: the auction put stronger ads on top. If you read the naive 2.8× as "position bias," you'd over-correct and end up inflating low-slot ads.

A newly launched ad is genuinely excellent, but the model's initial prediction for it is low. There's no exploration. What most likely happens?

  • The model quickly learns it's excellent from its first clicks
  • It rarely wins, collects almost no data, and the low prediction persists
  • The advertiser's bid automatically rises until it wins
  • The calibration layer corrects it

That's the feedback loop, in all its glory. No impressions, no labels. No labels, no change in the prediction. The remedies: an exploration budget, optimistic priors for new ads, or new-ad boosts with their own measurement.

It's the last month of the quarter and revenue is 2% short of target. A leader suggests turning Pebble's 5% exploration traffic off "just until the quarter closes." What do you say?

  • Quantify the ask. What does exploration actually cost? Measure it directly: explored requests vs. a matched sample of non-explored ones. It's often way less than the traffic share suggests, because explored impressions still earn some revenue and often get a floor price.
  • Name the delayed cost. Models retrained over the next few weeks will learn from logs with no exploration. New ads and advertisers get less data (cold start gets worse), and the feedback loop tightens. The bill arrives next quarter and is really hard to trace back. It's like raiding your savings account and hoping nobody checks.
  • Offer alternatives. Reduce exploration instead of killing it, target it where information is most valuable (new ads, uncertain segments), or use cheaper forms like slot shuffling, which costs very little but keeps position-bias estimates alive.
  • Make it explicit. If the call is to pause, write down an end date and an owner, and track new-ad performance and model calibration so the cost shows up somewhere.

What you built

Your stack can now measure position bias with a randomized experiment, correct for it with IPW, and explore at a rate you chose. Every log it collects from here on carries the consequences of that choice. No pressure.

So now we have logs, labels, and a healthy distrust of both. Next up, we have to decide what the model actually gets to see about a user and an ad. That's features, and it's where your product intuition finally gets to shine.
